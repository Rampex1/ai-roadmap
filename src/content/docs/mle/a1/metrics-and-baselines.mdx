In [F7](/foundations/f7/classification-metrics/) you learned *how* to compute precision, recall and
ROC AUC. This lesson is about *which* number to optimise, what "good enough" means, and what to compare
against. Zinkevich's Rule #2 is "First, design and implement metrics": decide how you will measure
success before you build the thing you will measure.

## Three layers of metrics

Think of metrics in three layers, from what the business cares about to what you can compute on a
laptop:

1. **Business objective.** The number leadership watches: monthly fraud losses, revenue retained,
   support cost. It moves slowly and is affected by many things besides your model.
2. **Online (product) metrics.** Measured on live traffic, usually in an experiment: fraud loss per
   £1,000 of transactions, the share of legitimate payments blocked, how many cases reviewers handle
   per hour, time to resolution. Alongside the metric you want to improve, track **guardrail metrics**
   that must not get worse: latency, complaint rate, the share of good customers inconvenienced.
3. **Offline metrics.** Computed on held-out historical data before launch: precision and recall at
   the chosen threshold, expected cost, precision in the top $k$, log loss. They are fast and cheap,
   so they drive day-to-day modelling. Their only job is to **predict** the online result.

The gap between layers is where projects go wrong. A model can improve offline AUC and not change
losses at all, because the threshold is in the wrong place, because reviewers can only look at 200
cases a day, or because fraudsters move. Offline metrics are a <Term id="proxy-metric" />: useful only
as long as they track the thing you care about.

:::note[Online measurement in one paragraph]
The standard way to measure the online effect is an <Term id="a-b-test" />: randomly send some traffic to
the new system and some to the old, then compare the online metrics. Randomisation makes the groups
comparable, so a difference can be attributed to the system. Designing and reading such tests properly
(sample size, duration, many metrics at once) belongs to A5 Evaluation.
:::

## Choose the offline metric from the decision

The decision from lesson 2 tells you which offline metric to use:

| How predictions are used | Offline metric that matches |
|---|---|
| Act on everything above a threshold; errors have known costs | expected cost (or value) at the chosen threshold |
| A fixed capacity, for example reviewers check 200 cases a day | precision in the top $k$ (precision@k), recall in the top $k$ |
| A requirement such as "catch at least 80% of fraud" | precision at the threshold that reaches 80% recall |
| The probability itself is used (pricing, expected loss) | log loss, and <Term id="calibration" /> checks |
| Ranking many items for a user | ranking metrics on the top of the list |

ROC AUC summarises ranking quality over *all* thresholds, which makes it a handy model-comparison number
early on. But you never deploy all thresholds; you deploy one. Report the metric at the operating point.

## Costs, the cost matrix and the threshold

When different errors have different costs, write them in a <Term id="cost-matrix" />. For fraud, say a
missed fraud (false negative) costs on average $c_{FN} = £150$ and a false alarm (false positive) costs
$c_{FP} = £5$ in call-centre time and annoyed customers. Correct decisions cost nothing extra:

| | predicted fraud | predicted legitimate |
|---|---|---|
| **actually fraud** | 0 | $c_{FN} = 150$ |
| **actually legitimate** | $c_{FP} = 5$ | 0 |

The total cost of flagging at threshold $t$ is

$$
\text{cost}(t) = c_{FP} \cdot FP(t) + c_{FN} \cdot FN(t)
$$

where $FP(t)$ and $FN(t)$ are the numbers of false positives and false negatives when everything with
predicted probability $\ge t$ is flagged.

There is a neat theoretical answer for the threshold. For one transaction with predicted fraud
probability $p$, flagging costs $c_{FP}(1 - p)$ in expectation (it's a false alarm with probability
$1 - p$), and not flagging costs $c_{FN} \cdot p$. Flag when the first is smaller:

$$
c_{FP}(1 - p) < c_{FN} \cdot p \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p > t^* = \frac{c_{FP}}{c_{FP} + c_{FN}}
$$

With £5 and £150, $t^* = 5/155 \approx 0.032$: flag anything with more than a 3.2% chance of being
fraud. That is a long way from the default 0.5. The formula assumes the probabilities are
**calibrated**; if they are not, choose the threshold by scanning candidates on validation data and
picking the cheapest, which you will do in the exercises.

## Baselines

A score only means something next to a <Term id="baseline-model" />. Use three kinds:

1. **Trivial:** do nothing, do everything, or predict the majority class. For fraud, "flag nothing"
   costs $c_{FN}$ times the number of frauds, and "flag everything" costs $c_{FP}$ times the number of
   legitimate transactions.
2. **Heuristic:** the current rule or process (lesson 1's rule). This is the real competitor.
3. **Simple model:** a <Term id="logistic-regression" /> or a small tree ensemble on a few obvious
   features. If a complex model barely beats it, ship the simple one.

Here are all of them on the synthetic fraud data from lesson 1, scored by total cost on a held-out half:

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 20_000
df = pd.DataFrame({
    "amount": rng.lognormal(mean=4, sigma=1, size=n).round(2),
    "new_device": rng.random(n) < 0.1,
    "foreign": rng.random(n) < 0.05,
})
logit = -7.5 + 0.7 * np.log(df["amount"]) + 2.0 * df["new_device"] + 1.5 * df["foreign"]
df["fraud"] = rng.random(n) < 1 / (1 + np.exp(-logit))

X = np.column_stack([np.log(df["amount"]), df["new_device"], df["foreign"]])
y = df["fraud"].to_numpy()
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te, df_tr, df_te = train_test_split(X, y, df, test_size=0.5, random_state=0, stratify=y)

C_FP, C_FN = 5, 150   # a false alarm costs £5, a missed fraud £150

def cost(y_true, flags):
    fp = np.sum(flags & ~y_true)
    fn = np.sum(~flags & y_true)
    return C_FP * fp + C_FN * fn

p = LogisticRegression().fit(X_tr, y_tr).predict_proba(X_te)[:, 1]
rule = ((df_te["amount"] > 200) & (df_te["new_device"] | df_te["foreign"])).to_numpy()
policies = {
    "flag nothing": np.zeros_like(y_te),
    "flag everything": np.ones_like(y_te),
    "rule": rule,
    "model, t = 0.5": p >= 0.5,
    "model, t = 0.032": p >= C_FP / (C_FP + C_FN),
}
for name, flags in policies.items():
    print(f"{name:17s} flags {flags.mean():6.1%}   cost £{cost(y_te, flags):>7,}")
```

```text title="Output"
flag nothing      flags   0.0%   cost £ 27,000
flag everything   flags 100.0%   cost £ 49,100
rule              flags   1.6%   cost £ 22,220
model, t = 0.5    flags   0.0%   cost £ 27,005
model, t = 0.032  flags  12.4%   cost £ 16,615
```

Three lessons in five lines:

- **The model at the default threshold is useless.** With fraud this rare, almost no transaction gets
  a fraud probability above 0.5, so at $t = 0.5$ the model flags a single transaction, a false alarm
  (hence £27,005). The model is identical to the one on the last line, with the same ROC AUC (about
  0.80); only the decision rule differs.
- **The rule is a real competitor.** It saves £4,780 over doing nothing.
- **The model at the cost-based threshold wins,** saving £10,385 over doing nothing and £5,605 (about
  25%) over the rule, even though it flags 12% of transactions. Whether reviewers can handle that many
  flags is a separate question, a capacity constraint the framing must state.

## Setting a success threshold

Agree on what "success" means **before** you see model results, so that the bar isn't quietly moved to
wherever the model lands. A good success criterion:

- names the offline metric and the data it is computed on ("expected cost on the most recent full month,
  with matured labels");
- compares with the baseline ("at least 20% lower cost than the current rule");
- includes guardrails ("blocks no more than 2% of legitimate transactions; p99 latency under 100 ms");
- says what happens online ("then a two-week A/B test on 10% of traffic; launch if fraud loss per
  £1,000 falls and complaints don't rise").

:::caution[Goodhart's law]
"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Optimise only precision and the
model flags almost nothing; optimise reviewer throughput and reviewers rush. Pair every target metric
with guardrails that catch the obvious way to game it.
:::

## Estimating a baseline before you have a model

Some baselines can be computed with no model at all, which is useful when deciding whether a project is
worth starting:

- A **random** ranking has expected precision@k equal to the base rate: if 2% of cases are fraud,
  reviewing 200 random cases finds about 4.
- "Flag nothing" and "flag everything" costs follow directly from the class counts.
- The current process's metrics usually exist in logs; if not, measure them on a labelled sample.

If the best you can hope for (say, perfect recall at the capacity limit) is barely better than the
baseline, no model will rescue the project.

## Summary

- Metrics come in layers: business objective, online product metrics with guardrails, and offline
  metrics whose job is to predict the online result.
- Pick the offline metric from the decision: expected cost at a threshold, precision@k under capacity,
  log loss when probabilities are used directly.
- With a cost matrix, flag when $p > c_{FP}/(c_{FP} + c_{FN})$ for calibrated probabilities, or scan
  thresholds on validation data and take the cheapest.
- Compare against trivial, heuristic and simple-model baselines; the heuristic is usually the one to beat.
- Write the success threshold, with guardrails, before seeing model results.
