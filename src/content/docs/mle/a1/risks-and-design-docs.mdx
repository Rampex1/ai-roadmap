The previous lessons produced a framing, metrics, baselines and a system sketch. Before building,
collect the **constraints**: what the system must do besides being accurate, and what happens when it
goes wrong. Then put everything on one page that other people can review. Problems found on a page cost
minutes to fix; the same problems found in production cost weeks, money or trust.

## Requirements are numbers with owners

"It should be fast and fair" is not a requirement. A requirement says what is measured, the limit, and
who agreed to it. The usual categories:

### Latency and throughput

Online systems have a latency budget: a card payment network might give the fraud check a small slice
of the total time for a payment. Describe latency with **percentiles**, not the mean, because users
feel the slow tail:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
# 10,000 measured request latencies in milliseconds: mostly fast, with a slow tail.
latency = np.concatenate([rng.normal(40, 8, 9_800), rng.normal(400, 100, 200)])
print(f"mean {latency.mean():.0f} ms")
for q in [50, 95, 99]:
    print(f"p{q}  {np.percentile(latency, q):.0f} ms")
```

```text title="Output"
mean 47 ms
p50  40 ms
p95  55 ms
p99  394 ms
```

The mean of 47 ms looks fine; one request in a hundred takes about 400 ms. If the budget is "p99 under
100 ms", this system fails. Throughput (requests per second at peak, from lesson 4) completes the
picture.

### Cost

Count all of it: compute for training and serving, storage, labelling (human reviewers are often the
largest item), and the engineers who maintain the system. Express it per prediction or per month and
compare with the value estimate from lesson 1. A model that is 1% better but needs a GPU per request
may not pay.

### Privacy

From [F11 privacy](/foundations/f11/privacy/): use only the data the task needs (data minimisation),
check you have a lawful basis and consent for this use, know where personal data flows and how long it
is kept, and remember that models can memorise training examples. Removing names is not anonymisation.
Write down which personal fields the model uses and why each is needed.

### Fairness

From [F11 measuring fairness](/foundations/f11/measuring-fairness/): decide which groups to check,
which metric matters for this decision (for a fraud block, the false positive rate per group is the
harm to legitimate customers; for a loan, approval rates and error rates), and the largest acceptable
gap. Proxies matter: a model without a protected attribute can still use postcode or device type as a
<Term id="proxy-variable" text="proxy" />. And the impossibility results in
[F11 calibration and trade-offs](/foundations/f11/calibration-and-trade-offs/) mean you must *choose*
which criterion to prioritise; write down the choice and the reason.

### Explainability

Who needs an explanation, and of what? A reviewer wants to know *why this case was flagged* ("new
device, amount ten times the usual"). A customer refused credit may be legally entitled to the main
reasons; rules on this vary by country and sector, so involve legal or compliance experts early (as of
late 2026, the EU AI Act, GDPR and US credit regulations all contain relevant provisions). Requirements
here can rule out model types, or require per-prediction reason codes, before any modelling starts.

## Failure modes and fallbacks

List how the system can fail and what happens then. A starter table for the fraud system:

| Failure | How you notice | What happens meanwhile |
|---|---|---|
| Model service down or too slow | health checks, latency alerts | fall back to the rule; never block all payments |
| Missing or malformed input | input validation | default values or the rule, and an alert |
| Upstream data change (a field renamed, units changed) | data quality checks | block deployment; page the data owner |
| Gradual drift (new fraud patterns) | drift and prediction-distribution monitors | scheduled retraining; review threshold |
| Adversaries adapt to the model | rising losses in a segment | rules for the new pattern; faster retraining |
| Feedback loop starves labels | label volume per segment | random holdout keeps labels arriving |
| Unfair errors for a group | per-group metrics in monitoring | threshold review; escalation owner |

Two principles fall out of this table. **Always have a fallback** that does something sensible without
the model: usually the rule from lesson 1, which is one more reason to build it first. And **fail
safe**: decide in advance whether an outage should block or allow (for fraud, usually allow small
payments and hold large ones), instead of discovering the behaviour during the outage.

:::tip[Human review as a safety valve]
Sending uncertain or high-stakes cases to a person reduces the cost of model errors. It also produces
labels. Its limits are capacity (the precision@k metric from lesson 3) and automation bias: reviewers
tend to agree with the model, so measure how often they overturn it.
:::

## The one-page ML design doc

A design doc is a short document, written before building, that lets colleagues spot problems early.
For an ML system, one page (or two) with these headings is enough:

1. **Problem and objective.** The business goal and the metric that should move.
2. **Decision and users.** What decision the predictions drive, who acts on them, and the capacity.
3. **ML task.** Prediction target, unit of prediction, prediction time, label definition, label delay,
   and known proxy gaps.
4. **Data.** Sources, owners, volumes, which personal data, how labels are obtained.
5. **Metrics and success criteria.** Offline metric that matches the decision, online metrics and
   guardrails, and the success threshold agreed in advance.
6. **Baselines.** Trivial, heuristic and simple model, with their numbers.
7. **System sketch.** Batch or online, features and their freshness, training cadence, feedback and
   logging, monitoring layers.
8. **Risks and requirements.** Latency, cost, privacy, fairness, explainability, and the failure-mode
   table with fallbacks.
9. **Rollout plan.** Shadow mode (the model runs but its decisions aren't applied), then an A/B test,
   then gradual rollout, with the criteria to stop.
10. **Open questions.** What you don't know yet and who will answer it.

Here is a compressed example for the fraud system:

> **Objective:** cut card fraud losses (about £2.7 million a month before any rule, £2.2 million with the current rule, on 1 million transactions) without raising complaints.
> **Decision:** at payment time, approve, or hold and send an SMS check. Reviewer capacity is not
> needed for SMS holds. **Task:** per transaction, P(fraud), label = confirmed dispute within 90 days
> (median delay 20 days; 99th percentile under 90). Proxy gap: undisputed fraud is labelled legitimate.
> **Data:** transactions, device, account history; no protected attributes; postcode excluded pending a
> proxy check. **Metrics:** offline expected cost with £150 per missed fraud and £5 per hold, on the
> latest month with matured labels; online loss per £1,000 transacted, with guardrails of hold rate
> ≤ 2% of legitimate payments and p99 latency ≤ 100 ms. **Success:** at least 20% lower expected cost
> than the current rule. **Baselines:** flag nothing £27.0k, rule £22.2k, logistic regression
> £16.6k per 10,000 transactions. **System:** online scoring, hourly batch account features plus
> real-time amount and device features; weekly retraining; 1% random holdout. **Risks:** service
> outage falls back to the rule; hold-rate gap between age bands ≤ 1 percentage point; reason codes
> for every hold. **Rollout:** two weeks shadow, then A/B on 10% of traffic. **Open:** can disputes
> be labelled faster from customer SMS replies?

Notice that nothing in it depends on which algorithm you pick. That is the point: the doc settles what
must be true of *any* model, so the modelling work that follows has a target.

## Summary

- Requirements are measurable limits with owners: latency percentiles and throughput, cost per
  prediction, privacy, per-group fairness gaps, and explainability needs.
- Describe latency with percentiles; the mean hides the tail users feel.
- List failure modes with how you detect each and the fallback; the rule-based baseline is the natural
  fallback, and outages should fail safe.
- A one-page ML design doc (problem, decision, task, data, metrics, baselines, system, risks, rollout,
  open questions) is written before modelling and reviewed by others. The homework asks you to write
  one, with a working baseline and a cost-based threshold.
