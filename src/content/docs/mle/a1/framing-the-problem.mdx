"Use ML to reduce churn" is a wish, not a task. A model needs a table: one row per *something*, features
known at *some moment*, and a <Term id="label" /> defined *precisely*. Framing is the work of turning the
wish into that table, and it decides more about the project's success than the choice of algorithm.

## Start from the decision

Work backwards through four links:

1. **Business objective.** What number should move? "Reduce monthly revenue lost to cancellations."
2. **Decision.** What will someone (or something) *do* differently? "Each week, choose which customers
   get a retention call." If no decision changes, a prediction has no value, however accurate.
3. **Prediction.** What would make that decision better? "For each active customer, the probability
   that they cancel in the next 30 days."
4. **Label.** What recorded fact tells us, afterwards, whether the prediction was right? "The account
   was closed within 30 days of the prediction date."

Writing the chain out exposes mismatches. Here, the decision is *who to call*, but the prediction is
*who will cancel*. Those are not the same: a customer who will cancel whatever you do is a wasted call,
and so is one who would have stayed anyway. What you really want is the customers whose behaviour
**the call changes**. Predicting that effect directly is called *uplift modelling*, and it needs data
from experiments (some customers called at random). A churn model is a reasonable first step, but the
framing should say out loud that it is a proxy.

:::caution[The target is a value judgement]
In [F11](/foundations/f11/why-responsible-ai/) you met a health algorithm that predicted *costs* as a
stand-in for *needs*, and so under-served patients who had received less care. The model fitted its
target well; the target was wrong. Always ask whether the thing you can label is the thing you care
about, and for whom it differs.
:::

## The unit of prediction

The **unit of prediction** is what one row, and one prediction, is about:

| Problem | Unit | One row means |
|---|---|---|
| Card fraud | transaction | "is this payment fraudulent?" |
| Churn | customer × week | "will this customer cancel in the 30 days after this Monday?" |
| Recommendations | user × item | "will this user click this item if shown?" |
| Demand forecasting | store × product × day | "how many units will sell?" |

The choice matters in three ways:

- **It sets the decision granularity.** A per-customer score cannot tell you which of their
  transactions to block.
- **It decides what counts as independent.** In the churn table the same customer appears every week.
  If one week of customer 17 lands in training and the next in test, the model is partly tested on a
  customer it has already seen. Split by customer or, better for forecasting, by time. This is the
  <Term id="data-leakage" /> problem from [F7](/foundations/f7/preprocessing-and-pipelines/), in its
  most common real-world form.
- **It decides how metrics are counted.** Precision per transaction and precision per customer can tell
  different stories when a few customers make most transactions.

## Prediction time and the feature cutoff

Every prediction happens at a moment, the **prediction time** $t$. Features may only use information
that was available *before* $t$; the label describes what happened *after* $t$. For churn with a 30-day
window:

- features: everything known up to the snapshot date $t$ (logins, payments, support tickets);
- label: did the customer cancel in $(t, t + 30\text{ days}]$?

Getting this wrong is the most common way to build a model that looks brilliant offline and fails in
production. A column like "number of support tickets in the last 30 days", computed *today* for a
snapshot taken two months ago, quietly includes tickets from after the snapshot, including the angry
"please cancel my account" ticket. Lesson 4 shows how to compute features at the right moment.

## Writing the label definition

A label definition must be precise enough that two engineers would compute the same column. Here is
the churn label computed from a login log, for one snapshot date:

```python
import pandas as pd

logins = pd.DataFrame({
    "customer": ["a", "a", "b", "c", "c", "c"],
    "date": pd.to_datetime(["2026-03-02", "2026-03-20", "2026-02-25", "2026-02-27", "2026-04-15", "2026-04-29"]),
})
snapshot = pd.Timestamp("2026-03-01")   # the moment we make the prediction
window_end = snapshot + pd.Timedelta(days=30)

in_window = logins[(logins["date"] > snapshot) & (logins["date"] <= window_end)]
customers = pd.Series(sorted(logins["customer"].unique()), name="customer")
labels = pd.DataFrame({"customer": customers,
                       "churned": ~customers.isin(in_window["customer"])})
print(labels)
print("labels complete after:", window_end.date())
```

```text title="Output"
  customer  churned
0        a    False
1        b     True
2        c     True
labels complete after: 2026-03-31
```

Look at customer `c`: no login in March, so they are labelled as churned, yet they came back on
15 April. Is that churn? It depends on the business: for a streaming service, a month away may really
be lost revenue; for an annual tax tool, it is normal. Edge cases like this must be decided and written
down:

- Which events count as "active" (a login? a payment? any app open)?
- What about paused accounts, downgrades, refunds, customers who join mid-window?
- Who is in the population at all (only customers active on the snapshot date)?
- How long is the window, and why that length (it should match how far ahead the decision needs to act)?

## Label delay

The <Term id="label-delay" /> is the time between making a prediction and being able to know the true
label. It is often longer than people expect:

- **Click prediction:** seconds to minutes.
- **Churn with a 30-day window:** at least 30 days, by definition.
- **Card fraud:** a fraud label usually arrives when the cardholder disputes the payment, which can take
  weeks or months.
- **Loan default:** months to years.

<Figure src={timelineImg} alt="A timeline. Features come from before the prediction time t. The label window runs from t to t plus 30 days. Labels for predictions made in the last 30 days are not known yet, so the newest training example must have a prediction time at least 30 days before today." caption="With a 30-day label window, the newest rows you can train or evaluate on are at least 30 days old." />

Label delay has three consequences:

1. **Training data are always somewhat stale.** With a 30-day window, the freshest labelled rows are a
   month old. Including newer rows with a "not churned yet" label would be wrong: they simply haven't
   had time to churn.
2. **You can't see performance quickly.** If the model degrades today, accuracy-based monitoring
   notices weeks later. Teams watch faster *proxy* signals in the meantime, such as the distribution of
   inputs and predictions (A10 Monitoring and drift covers this).
3. **It limits how fast you can iterate.** An A/B test of a churn intervention needs to run at least as
   long as the label window, plus the time to collect enough customers.

When labels trickle in, as with fraud disputes, measure the delay distribution rather than guess it.
Here are 500 synthetic fraud cases with the transaction time and the time the fraud was reported:

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n = 500
tx_time = pd.Timestamp("2026-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 90, n), unit="D")
delay_days = rng.gamma(shape=2.0, scale=12.0, size=n).round()   # most chargebacks arrive in weeks
frauds = pd.DataFrame({"tx_time": tx_time, "label_time": tx_time + pd.to_timedelta(delay_days, unit="D")})

delay = (frauds["label_time"] - frauds["tx_time"]).dt.days
print(f"median delay: {delay.median():.0f} days")
print(f"90th percentile: {delay.quantile(0.9):.0f} days")
print(f"known within 30 days: {(delay <= 30).mean():.0%}")
print(f"known within 60 days: {(delay <= 60).mean():.0%}")
```

```text title="Output"
median delay: 20 days
90th percentile: 44 days
known within 30 days: 72%
known within 60 days: 97%
```

Even for transactions exactly 30 days old, about 28% of the frauds have not been reported yet; for last
week's transactions, most have not. If you trained on recent rows, those frauds would be labelled
legitimate. The model would learn that some fraud patterns are fine, and evaluation would understate
recall. A common rule: only use rows whose labels are "mature", for example older than the 95th or 99th
percentile of the delay.

## Choosing the task type

Once target, unit and label are fixed, the ML task type usually follows:

- **Binary classification:** churn in 30 days, fraud, default.
- **Regression:** next month's spend, delivery time, units sold.
- **Ranking:** which 10 items to show, which 200 cases to review today. Often built from a classifier's
  scores, but evaluated on the top of the list (lesson 3).
- **No supervised task at all:** if there are no labels, you may start with
  <Term id="unsupervised-learning" text="unsupervised" /> methods (clustering, anomaly scores) and a
  labelling plan.

Sometimes the same goal can be framed several ways. "How long until this customer leaves?" can be a
regression, a set of classifiers (30, 60, 90 days) or a survival model. Pick the framing whose output
plugs most directly into the decision.

## A framing checklist

Before writing any modelling code, you should be able to fill in:

| Item | Churn example |
|---|---|
| Business objective | reduce revenue lost to cancellations |
| Decision driven | weekly list of customers to call (capacity: 500 calls) |
| Prediction | P(cancel within 30 days), per customer, every Monday |
| Unit of prediction | customer × week, active customers only |
| Label definition | account closed in $(t, t + 30\text{d}]$; pauses don't count |
| Label delay | 30 days (by definition) |
| Features allowed | anything known before Monday 00:00 |
| Known proxy gap | predicts churn, not "persuadable by a call" |

## Summary

- Work backwards: business objective → decision → prediction → label. If no decision changes, stop.
- The unit of prediction is what one row is about; it fixes the decision granularity, how to split
  without leakage and how metrics are counted.
- Features come from before the prediction time; the label from a window after it.
- Write the label definition precisely, including edge cases, and check whether it is a proxy for what
  you really care about.
- Label delay makes training data stale, slows monitoring and experiments. Measure its distribution and
  only train on rows whose labels have matured.
