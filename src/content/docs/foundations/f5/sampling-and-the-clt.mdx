In practice you never see a whole distribution. You see a **sample**: 500 survey answers, 10,000 test images,
one minibatch of 64 examples. This lesson answers two questions that every later lesson relies on. Does the
average of a sample settle on the true average? And how far off is it likely to be?

## Population, sample and the sample mean

The **population** (or the underlying distribution) is the full set of possible values, with a true mean
$\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$ that you do not know. A **sample** is $n$ values $X_1, \dots, X_n$ drawn
from it, which we will assume are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d., from
[lesson 2](/foundations/f5/conditional-probability/)). Their average is the **sample mean**:

$$
\bar{X} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i
$$

The key shift in thinking: **$\bar{X}$ is itself a random variable**. Draw a different sample and you get a
different $\bar{X}$. So it has its own distribution, with its own mean and spread, and we can study them.

## The law of large numbers

The <Term id="law-of-large-numbers" /> says that as $n$ grows, the sample mean gets closer and closer to the
true mean: $\bar{X} \to \mu$. This is why long-run frequencies work as probabilities, and why simulation works
at all. Watch the running mean of die rolls approach 3.5:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
rolls = rng.integers(1, 7, size=100_000)
running_mean = np.cumsum(rolls) / np.arange(1, len(rolls) + 1)
for n in [10, 100, 1_000, 10_000, 100_000]:
    print(f"{n:>7,}  {running_mean[n - 1]:.4f}")
```

```text title="Output"
     10  2.8000
    100  3.6200
  1,000  3.6000
 10,000  3.5045
100,000  3.4980
```

`np.cumsum` gives the running totals, and dividing by $1, 2, 3, \dots$ turns them into running means. Early on
the mean jumps around; later it hardly moves.

:::caution[The law of large numbers is not "the law of averages"]
After ten tails in a row, a fair coin is **not** more likely to land heads. The coin has no memory. The running
mean recovers because the early streak gets **diluted** by thousands of ordinary flips, not because later flips
compensate. Believing in compensation is the **gambler's fallacy**.
:::

## How far off? The standard error

Using the variance rules from [lesson 4](/foundations/f5/random-variables/): the sum of $n$ independent values
has variance $n\sigma^2$, and dividing by $n$ divides the variance by $n^2$. So

$$
\mathbb{E}[\bar{X}] = \mu, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}, \qquad \operatorname{SD}(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

The standard deviation of an estimate has a special name, the <Term id="standard-error" /> (SE). For a mean it
is $\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$. Check it by simulation: draw 20,000 samples of size $n$ from an **exponential**
distribution (a skewed distribution of waiting times, here with $\mu = 1$ and $\sigma = 1$), take each
sample's mean, and measure the spread of those means:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
for n in [4, 16, 64, 256]:
    samples = rng.exponential(scale=1.0, size=(20_000, n))   # 20,000 samples of size n
    means = samples.mean(axis=1)                             # one mean per sample
    print(f"n={n:>3}  mean of means {means.mean():.3f}  sd of means {means.std():.4f}  1/sqrt(n) {1 / np.sqrt(n):.4f}")
```

```text title="Output"
n=  4  mean of means 0.995  sd of means 0.4966  1/sqrt(n) 0.5000
n= 16  mean of means 0.997  sd of means 0.2490  1/sqrt(n) 0.2500
n= 64  mean of means 1.000  sd of means 0.1255  1/sqrt(n) 0.1250
n=256  mean of means 1.000  sd of means 0.0625  1/sqrt(n) 0.0625
```

Each row is centred on $\mu = 1$, and the spread matches $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$. Notice the pattern: **four times
the data halves the error**. That square root is why the simulation in lesson 1 needed a hundred times more runs
for each extra decimal place.

## The central limit theorem

The standard error says how wide the distribution of $\bar{X}$ is. The <Term id="central-limit-theorem" />
(CLT) says what **shape** it has:

> For i.i.d. values with finite variance, the sample mean is approximately normal for large $n$, whatever the
> shape of the original distribution: $\bar{X} \approx \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)$.

The exponential distribution is far from a bell curve: most values are small, with a long tail to the right. Its
sample means start out skewed but quickly become bell-shaped:

One way to measure the remaining skew: for a symmetric distribution, half the sample means fall below the true
mean.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
for n in [1, 5, 30, 200]:
    means = rng.exponential(scale=1.0, size=(20_000, n)).mean(axis=1)
    print(f"n={n:>3}  fraction below the mean: {np.mean(means < 1):.3f}")
```

```text title="Output"
n=  1  fraction below the mean: 0.636
n=  5  fraction below the mean: 0.562
n= 30  fraction below the mean: 0.523
n=200  fraction below the mean: 0.506
```

The fraction moves towards 0.5 as $n$ grows. How large $n$ must be depends on how skewed the data are; for
mildly skewed data, a few dozen is often enough. Strongly skewed or heavy-tailed data need more.

:::note[When it fails]
The CLT needs a finite variance. For extremely heavy-tailed distributions (the Cauchy distribution is the
textbook example) a single huge value can dominate any average, and the sample mean never settles. Real data with
rare, enormous values (insurance claims, viral posts) can behave badly in the same way.
:::

The CLT is why the normal distribution is everywhere: anything that is an average or a sum of many small,
roughly independent pieces tends to look normal. It is also what makes the confidence intervals and tests of
the next lessons work, because it tells us that $\bar{X}$ lands within about $1.96$ standard errors of $\mu$
95% of the time.

## Why this matters for ML

- **Minibatch gradients are sample means.** The gradient on a batch of $B$ examples estimates the full-data
  gradient with a standard error proportional to $\frac{1}{\sqrt{B}}$. Quadrupling the batch only halves the
  noise, which is one reason very large batches give diminishing returns.
- **Test-set metrics are sample means.** Accuracy on 1,000 test examples is a mean of 0/1 values. With accuracy
  near 0.9, its standard error is $\sqrt{0.9 \times 0.1 / 1000} \approx 0.0095$, so two models at 90.2% and
  90.8% may not really differ.
- **Averaging over random seeds** reduces noise in experiment results the same way.

## Summary

- A sample mean $\bar{X}$ is a random variable: a new sample gives a new value.
- Law of large numbers: $\bar{X} \to \mu$ as $n$ grows. Early streaks get diluted, not compensated.
- Standard error of the mean: $\sigma / \sqrt{n}$. Four times the data halves the error.
- Central limit theorem: for finite variance, $\bar{X}$ is approximately $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2 / n)$ for
  large $n$, whatever the original shape.
- Minibatch gradients and test metrics are sample means, with the same $1 / \sqrt{n}$ noise.
