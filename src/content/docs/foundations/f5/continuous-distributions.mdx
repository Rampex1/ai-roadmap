Heights, temperatures, sensor readings and the weights of a neural network are not counts. They can take any
value in a range, and a pmf that lists every value is impossible. This lesson moves from sums to areas, using
the integrals from [F4](/foundations/f4/integrals/), and meets the single most important distribution in
statistics, the normal.

## Density, not probability

Pick a number uniformly at random between 2 and 6. What is the probability that it is **exactly** 3? There are
infinitely many possible values, so the answer is 0. A simulation agrees:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = rng.uniform(2, 6, size=100_000)       # Uniform(2, 6)
print(round(((x > 3) & (x < 4)).mean(), 4))
print(round(x.mean(), 3), round(x.var(), 3))
print(np.mean(x == 3.0))
```

```text title="Output"
0.2508
3.998 1.331
0.0
```

None of the 100,000 draws is exactly 3.0, but about a quarter land between 3 and 4. For a **continuous**
random variable, only intervals have positive probability. They come from a
<Term id="probability-density" /> $p(x)$, a curve whose **area** over an interval is the probability:

$$
P(a < X < b) = \int_a^b p(x)\,dx
$$

A density is never negative and has total area 1. The **cumulative distribution function** (CDF),
$F(x) = P(X \le x)$, is the area to the left of $x$, which turns interval probabilities into a subtraction:
$P(a < X \le b) = F(b) - F(a)$. Because single points have probability 0, it makes no difference whether you
write $<$ or $\le$.

Expected value and variance work as before, with integrals in place of sums:
$\mathbb{E}[X] = \int x\,p(x)\,dx$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X) = \int (x - \mu)^2\,p(x)\,dx$.

:::caution[A density value is not a probability]
$p(x)$ can be larger than 1. A normal curve with a small spread, $\sigma = 0.1$, peaks at about 3.99. Only
areas are probabilities, and the area stays 1:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

print(round(stats.norm.pdf(0, loc=0, scale=0.1), 3))
grid = np.linspace(-1, 1, 20001)
print(round(np.trapezoid(stats.norm.pdf(grid, scale=0.1), grid), 4))
```

```text title="Output"
3.989
1.0
```
:::

## The uniform distribution

**Uniform**($a$, $b$) spreads probability evenly over $[a, b]$: its density is the constant $\frac{1}{b - a}$
there and 0 elsewhere, a flat rectangle of area 1. Probabilities are rectangle areas, so for Uniform(2, 6),
$P(3 < X < 4) = 1 \times \frac{1}{4} = 0.25$. Its mean is the midpoint $\frac{a + b}{2} = 4$ and its variance
is $\frac{(b - a)^2}{12} = \frac{16}{12} \approx 1.333$, matching the simulation above. `rng.random()` is
Uniform(0, 1), the building block for simulating everything else.

## The normal (Gaussian) distribution

The <Term id="normal-distribution" /> $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ is the bell curve. It has two parameters,
the mean $\mu$ (the centre) and the standard deviation $\sigma$ (the width):

$$
p(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)
$$

You will rarely type this formula, but notice its shape: the further $x$ is from $\mu$, measured in units of
$\sigma$, the faster the density falls off, because the distance is squared inside the exponential. The
fraction $\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}$ is what makes the total area exactly 1.

The **standard normal** is $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, with mean 0 and standard deviation 1. Its CDF has no simple
formula, so we use `scipy.stats.norm` (or a table). The famous **68-95-99.7 rule**:

```python
from scipy import stats

for k in [1, 2, 3]:
    print(k, round(stats.norm.cdf(k) - stats.norm.cdf(-k), 4))
```

```text title="Output"
1 0.6827
2 0.9545
3 0.9973
```

For any normal distribution, about 68% of values lie within one standard deviation of the mean, 95% within
two, and 99.7% within three. Something more than 3 standard deviations out happens about 3 times in 1,000.

### Why the normal is everywhere

- **Sums of many small effects look normal.** Height depends on many genes and environmental factors that add
  up. The central limit theorem (next lesson) says such sums tend towards a normal shape, whatever the
  individual effects look like.
- **Measurement noise** is often close to normal for the same reason.
- **It is convenient.** Sums of independent normals are normal, and the formula is easy to work with. In ML,
  assuming normal noise is what turns "find the most likely parameters" into "minimise squared error"
  (lesson 8), and network weights are often initialised by drawing them from a normal distribution.

It is not universal. Incomes, waiting times and file sizes are skewed with long right tails; a normal model
would badly underestimate how often extreme values occur.

## Standardising: z-scores

Any normal variable can be turned into a standard normal by subtracting its mean and dividing by its standard
deviation:

$$
z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}
$$

The **z-score** $z$ says how many standard deviations $x$ is from the mean. If adult heights are roughly
$\mathcal{N}(170, 10^2)$ in centimetres, a height of 185 cm has $z = \frac{185 - 170}{10} = 1.5$, and

$$
P(X > 185) = P(Z > 1.5) = 1 - F(1.5) \approx 0.067
$$

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

mu, sigma = 170, 10
z = (185 - mu) / sigma
print(z, round(1 - stats.norm.cdf(z), 4), round(stats.norm.sf(185, loc=mu, scale=sigma), 4))
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
heights = rng.normal(mu, sigma, size=100_000)
print(round((heights > 185).mean(), 4))
```

```text title="Output"
1.5 0.0668 0.0668
0.0661
```

- `stats.norm.cdf(x, loc=mu, scale=sigma)` is $F(x)$; with no `loc` and `scale` it is the standard normal.
  Note that `scale` is the standard deviation $\sigma$, not the variance.
- `stats.norm.sf(x)` is the "survival function" $1 - F(x)$, the area to the right.
- `stats.norm.ppf(q)` goes backwards: the value with area $q$ to its left. `stats.norm.ppf(0.975)` is about
  1.96, the cut-off that leaves 2.5% in each tail, so 95% lies within $\pm 1.96$. You will meet 1.96 again in
  confidence intervals.

The same subtraction and division works on data, normal or not. In [F2](/foundations/f2/broadcasting/) you
standardised columns with `(X - X.mean(axis=0)) / X.std(axis=0)`. The result has mean 0 and standard
deviation 1:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
salaries = rng.normal(40_000, 12_000, size=5)
z = (salaries - salaries.mean()) / salaries.std()
print(salaries.round(0))
print(z.round(2), round(z.mean(), 10), round(z.std(), 10))
```

```text title="Output"
[42269. 33727. 35043. 10702. 61596.]
[ 0.34 -0.18 -0.1  -1.59  1.52] 0.0 1.0
```

:::tip[Why ML standardises features]
Features on very different scales (income in tens of thousands, age in tens) make gradient descent zigzag and
let distance-based methods be dominated by the large-scale feature. Standardising puts every feature in the
same units, "standard deviations from the mean". Compute the mean and standard deviation on the **training**
data only, and reuse them for the test data.
:::

## Summary

- A continuous random variable has probability 0 at any single value; probabilities are areas under a density,
  $P(a < X < b) = \int_a^b p(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a)$.
- A density can exceed 1; only its areas are probabilities, and the total area is 1.
- Uniform($a$, $b$): flat density $\frac{1}{b - a}$, mean $\frac{a + b}{2}$, variance $\frac{(b - a)^2}{12}$.
- Normal $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$: the bell curve; 68%, 95% and 99.7% within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations.
- $z = (x - \mu)/\sigma$ standardises; `scipy.stats.norm` gives `pdf`, `cdf`, `sf` and `ppf`, with `scale` = $\sigma$.
