The last lesson warned that $P(A \mid B)$ and $P(B \mid A)$ are different. Often you know one and need the
other. A test's maker tells you $P(\text{positive} \mid \text{disease})$; a patient wants
$P(\text{disease} \mid \text{positive})$. <Term id="bayes-theorem" /> converts one into the other. It is
one line of algebra, and it changes how you read every test, alarm and classifier score.

## One line of algebra

The multiplication rule from the [last lesson](/foundations/f5/conditional-probability/) can be written two ways:

$$
P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)\,P(B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A)
$$

Divide the right-hand equality by $P(B)$:

$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}
$$

That is Bayes' theorem. Read with $A$ as a hypothesis ("has the disease") and $B$ as evidence ("tested
positive"), each part has a name:

| Part | Name | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| $P(A)$ | **prior** | how plausible the hypothesis was before the evidence |
| $P(B \mid A)$ | **likelihood** | how expected the evidence is if the hypothesis is true |
| $P(B)$ | **evidence** | how expected the evidence is overall |
| $P(A \mid B)$ | **posterior** | how plausible the hypothesis is after the evidence |

The bottom line, $P(B)$, usually comes from the law of total probability, splitting on whether $A$ is true:

$$
P(B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A) + P(B \mid A^c)\,P(A^c)
$$

## A medical test

A disease affects 1% of people. A test for it has:

- **sensitivity** 90%: if you have the disease, it is positive with probability 0.90, $P(+ \mid D) = 0.90$;
- **specificity** 91%: if you do not, it is negative with probability 0.91, so the **false positive
  rate** is $P(+ \mid D^c) = 0.09$.

You test positive. How likely is it that you have the disease? Most people, including many doctors in
published surveys, guess something near 90%. Bayes says:

$$
P(D \mid +) = \frac{0.90 \times 0.01}{0.90 \times 0.01 + 0.09 \times 0.99} = \frac{0.009}{0.009 + 0.0891} \approx 0.092
$$

About **9%**. A positive result raises your chance from 1% to 9%, a big jump, but you are still
probably healthy.

### Natural frequencies make it obvious

The formula is correct but opaque. Counting people instead of multiplying probabilities makes the answer
feel right. Imagine 10,000 people:

<Figure
  src={treeImg}
  alt="A tree: 10,000 people split into 100 with the disease and 9,900 without. Of the 100, 90 test positive and 10 negative. Of the 9,900, 891 test positive and 9,009 negative. 90 out of the 981 positives have the disease, about 9 percent"
  caption="Natural frequencies: of 981 positive tests, only 90 come from people with the disease."

There are 90 true positives but 891 false positives, because the healthy group is 99 times larger. A small
false positive rate applied to a huge group swamps a high hit rate applied to a small one. Among all 981
positives, $\frac{90}{981} \approx 9\%$ are sick.

:::caution[The base-rate fallacy]
Ignoring the prior (the **base rate**, here the 1% prevalence) and reading $P(+ \mid D)$ as if it were
$P(D \mid +)$ is the **base-rate fallacy**. It shows up whenever the thing you are looking for is rare: fraud
detection, security alarms, screening for rare diseases, a "99% accurate" detector of anything uncommon.
:::

### Code it and check it by simulation

```python
def posterior(prior, sensitivity, specificity):
    """P(disease | positive test)."""
    false_pos_rate = 1 - specificity
    evidence = sensitivity * prior + false_pos_rate * (1 - prior)   # P(positive)
    return sensitivity * prior / evidence

print(round(posterior(0.01, 0.90, 0.91), 4))
print(round(posterior(0.10, 0.90, 0.91), 4))
print(round(posterior(0.50, 0.90, 0.91), 4))
```

```text title="Output"
0.0917
0.5263
0.9091
```

The same test means very different things depending on the prior. If 10% of the people tested have the
disease (say, only people with symptoms are tested), a positive result means about 53%; at 50% it means 91%.

A simulation of a million people gives the same answer without any formula:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 1_000_000
sick = rng.random(n) < 0.01                       # 1% prevalence
u = rng.random(n)
positive = np.where(sick, u < 0.90, u < 0.09)     # sensitivity 90%, false positive rate 9%
print(sick.sum(), positive.sum(), (sick & positive).sum())
print(round(sick[positive].mean(), 4))
```

```text title="Output"
9996 97850 8965
0.0916
```

- `rng.random(n) < 0.01` is `True` with probability 0.01: a standard way to simulate an event of a given
  probability.
- `np.where(cond, x, y)` picks from `x` where `cond` is `True` and from `y` elsewhere, so sick people test
  positive with probability 0.90 and healthy people with probability 0.09.
- `sick[positive].mean()` is $P(D \mid +)$, filtered exactly as in the last lesson.

## Updating step by step

Bayes' theorem chains. After one positive test your belief is 9.2%. If you take a **second, independent**
test and it is also positive, the old posterior becomes the new prior:

```python
p1 = posterior(0.01, 0.90, 0.91)       # after one positive test
p2 = posterior(p1, 0.90, 0.91)         # the posterior becomes the new prior
print(round(p1, 4), round(p2, 4))
```

```text title="Output"
0.0917 0.5025
```

Two positives take you to about 50%. Each piece of evidence moves the belief; none settles it alone. A
negative test updates too, using $P(- \mid D) = 0.10$ and $P(- \mid D^c) = 0.91$ in place of the positive
rates, and from a 1% prior it drops the probability to about 0.1%. (This step-by-step updating assumes the
tests' errors are independent given the disease status. Repeating the same flawed test on the same sample
usually breaks that.)

:::note[Bayesian thinking]
This is the core of **Bayesian** statistics: start with a prior belief, observe data, update to a posterior,
repeat. The 3Blue1Brown video in "Go deeper" draws the same idea as shrinking areas of a square, which many
people find the clearest picture of all.
:::

## Why this matters for ML

- **Classifier scores need base rates too.** A fraud model with 95% sensitivity and a 2% false positive rate
  sounds excellent, but if 1 transaction in 1,000 is fraud, most of its alerts are false alarms. Measuring
  *precision*, $P(\text{fraud} \mid \text{alert})$, on realistic data catches this; F7 and A5 return to it.
- **Class imbalance shifts outputs.** A model trained on balanced data (50% positives) has effectively learned
  with a prior of 50%. Deployed where positives are rare, its probabilities are too high unless corrected.
- **Naive Bayes** classifiers use the theorem directly: $P(\text{spam} \mid \text{words}) \propto P(\text{words} \mid \text{spam})\,P(\text{spam})$,
  where $\propto$ means "proportional to" (the bottom line is the same for both classes, so it can be skipped
  when comparing them).

## Summary

- Bayes' theorem: $P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{P(B \mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}$, with $P(B)$ from the law of total
  probability.
- Prior, likelihood, evidence, posterior: belief before, how well the hypothesis predicts the data, how
  likely the data are overall, belief after.
- With a 1% base rate, a test with 90% sensitivity and 91% specificity gives only about a 9% chance of
  disease after one positive. Ignoring the base rate is the base-rate fallacy.
- Natural frequencies (count 10,000 people) make Bayes problems easy to reason about.
- Posteriors chain: today's posterior is tomorrow's prior.
