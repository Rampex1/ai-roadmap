In [F7](/foundations/f7/what-is-machine-learning/) every model was a formula: a weighted sum of features (linear and logistic regression) or a vote of nearby
points (k-nearest neighbours). This module is about a different family that dominates real-world work on
**tabular data**, the spreadsheets and database tables most companies run on. It starts with a model you could draw
on a napkin: a flowchart of yes/no questions.

## A tree is a flowchart

A <Term id="decision-tree" /> asks one question about one feature at each **node**, such as "is the contract at most
10.5 months?", and follows the *yes* or *no* branch. When it reaches a **leaf** it stops and answers. Here is a
tree trained on ten customers of a phone company, predicting whether each one will **churn** (cancel) or stay:

<Figure
  src={treeImg}
  alt="A decision tree. Root: contract at most 10.5? No leads to a leaf predicting stay. Yes leads to a node asking charge at most 87.5? Yes leads to a leaf predicting churn, no to a leaf predicting stay"
  caption="A depth-2 tree for churn. Each box lists the training customers that reached it."
/>

To predict for a new customer with a 3-month contract paying 50 a month: $3 \le 10.5$, so go left; $50 \le 87.5$, so
go left again and land in a leaf where all 4 training customers churned. Prediction: **churn**. A customer on a
24-month contract goes right at the root and is predicted to stay, whatever they pay.

Some vocabulary:

- The **root** is the top node. The **depth** of a node is the number of questions above it; the depth of the tree
  is the depth of its deepest leaf (2 here).
- Each question compares **one feature with a threshold**. So every leaf owns a box-shaped region of feature space,
  with edges parallel to the axes.
- A classification leaf predicts the **majority class** of the training rows that reached it, and its class
  proportions serve as predicted probabilities. A regression leaf predicts the **mean** target of its rows.

Two things already stand out. The tree never multiplies or adds features, so it does not care whether charges are
in euros or cents: **no feature scaling needed** (compare the scaling you needed for [k-NN](/foundations/f7/k-nearest-neighbours/) and gradient descent). And
you can read the whole model, which is rare in machine learning.

## Where do the questions come from?

Training a tree means choosing the questions. The algorithm (called CART) is **greedy**: at the root, it tries every
feature and every threshold, picks the single split that makes the two children "purest", then repeats inside each
child. It never goes back to reconsider an earlier choice. To make "purest" precise we need a number.

## Impurity: how mixed is a node?

Let $p_k$ be the fraction of the node's rows in class $k$. A node is **pure** when one $p_k$ is 1. Two standard
impurity measures:

$$
\text{Gini}(p) = 1 - \sum_k p_k^2
\qquad\qquad
H(p) = -\sum_k p_k \log_2 p_k
$$

- **<Term id="gini-impurity" text="Gini impurity" />** is the probability that two rows drawn at random (with
  replacement) from the node have *different* classes. Pure node: $1 - 1^2 = 0$. A 50/50 two-class node:
  $1 - (0.25 + 0.25) = 0.5$, the maximum for two classes.
- **<Term id="entropy" />** is the average surprise from
  [F5 information theory](/foundations/f5/information-theory/): 0 bits for a pure node, 1 bit for a 50/50 node.

Both are 0 when pure and largest when the classes are evenly mixed. They almost always pick the same splits. Gini, scikit-learn's
default, is slightly cheaper to compute (no logarithm).

The root of our tree holds 6 stayers and 4 churners, so $p = (0.6, 0.4)$:

$$
\text{Gini} = 1 - (0.6^2 + 0.4^2) = 1 - 0.52 = 0.48
\qquad
H = -0.6\log_2 0.6 - 0.4\log_2 0.4 \approx 0.971 \text{ bits}
$$

## Scoring a split

A split sends $n_L$ of the node's $n$ rows left and $n_R$ right. Its quality is the impurity of the children,
weighted by how many rows each one gets:

$$
I_{\text{split}} = \frac{n_L}{n}\, I(\text{left}) + \frac{n_R}{n}\, I(\text{right})
$$

and the best split is the one with the biggest **decrease** $I(\text{parent}) - I_{\text{split}}$. With entropy as
$I$, that decrease is called <Term id="information-gain" />: how many bits of uncertainty about the class the
question removes, on average.

### A split by hand

Here are the ten customers:

| contract (months) | 1 | 3 | 6 | 6 | 9 | 12 | 12 | 24 | 24 | 36 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| charge | 85 | 40 | 90 | 75 | 55 | 45 | 30 | 80 | 60 | 50 |
| churn | 1 | 1 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |

**Split A: contract < 12.** Left gets the first five customers (4 churn, 1 stay); right gets the other five (all
stay).

- Left: $\text{Gini} = 1 - (0.8^2 + 0.2^2) = 0.32$. Right: pure, $\text{Gini} = 0$.
- Weighted: $\frac{5}{10}(0.32) + \frac{5}{10}(0) = 0.16$. Decrease: $0.48 - 0.16 = 0.32$.

**Split B: charge > 70.** Left gets the four big spenders (2 churn, 2 stay); right gets six (2 churn, 4 stay).

- Left: $\text{Gini} = 0.5$. Right: $1 - \left((\tfrac{1}{3})^2 + (\tfrac{2}{3})^2\right) = \tfrac{4}{9} \approx 0.444$.
- Weighted: $0.4(0.5) + 0.6(0.444) \approx 0.467$. Decrease: about $0.013$, almost nothing.

Split A wins by a mile. Let's check with code, using entropy too:

```python
import numpy as np

def gini(y):
    _, counts = np.unique(y, return_counts=True)
    p = counts / counts.sum()
    return 1 - np.sum(p ** 2)

def entropy(y):
    _, counts = np.unique(y, return_counts=True)
    p = counts / counts.sum()                 # np.unique only returns classes present, so no p = 0
    return -np.sum(p * np.log2(p))

churn    = np.array([1, 1, 0, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0])
contract = np.array([1, 3, 6, 6, 9, 12, 12, 24, 24, 36])
charge   = np.array([85, 40, 90, 75, 55, 45, 30, 80, 60, 50])

print(f"root: gini {gini(churn):.3f}, entropy {entropy(churn):.3f} bits")
for name, mask in [("contract < 12", contract < 12), ("charge > 70", charge > 70)]:
    left, right = churn[mask], churn[~mask]
    wl, wr = len(left) / len(churn), len(right) / len(churn)
    g = wl * gini(left) + wr * gini(right)
    h = wl * entropy(left) + wr * entropy(right)
    print(f"{name}: weighted gini {g:.3f} (decrease {gini(churn) - g:.3f}), "
          f"information gain {entropy(churn) - h:.3f} bits")
```

```text title="Output"
root: gini 0.480, entropy 0.971 bits
contract < 12: weighted gini 0.160 (decrease 0.320), information gain 0.610 bits
charge > 70: weighted gini 0.467 (decrease 0.013), information gain 0.020 bits
```

Both measures agree: knowing the contract length removes 0.61 of the 0.97 bits of uncertainty; knowing whether the
charge is above 70 removes almost none.

### Which thresholds to try?

For a numeric feature, only the **gaps between sorted distinct values** matter: any threshold between 9 and 12
splits the customers in exactly the same way. So the algorithm sorts the values and tries one threshold per gap,
conventionally the **midpoint**. For `contract` the candidates are 2, 4.5, 7.5, 10.5, 18 and 30; the winner,
between 9 and 12, becomes $10.5$. With $n$ rows and $d$ features that is at most $d(n - 1)$ candidates per node,
cheap enough to check them all.

## The same thing in scikit-learn

```python
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier, export_text

X = np.column_stack([contract, charge])
tree = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=2, random_state=0).fit(X, churn)
print(export_text(tree, feature_names=["contract", "charge"], show_weights=True))
print(tree.predict([[3, 50], [24, 95]]))
print(tree.predict_proba([[3, 50]]))
```

```text title="Output"
|--- contract <= 10.50
|   |--- charge <= 87.50
|   |   |--- weights: [0.00, 4.00] class: 1
|   |--- charge >  87.50
|   |   |--- weights: [1.00, 0.00] class: 0
|--- contract >  10.50
|   |--- weights: [5.00, 0.00] class: 0

[1 0]
[[0. 1.]]
```

`weights` are the class counts in each leaf (stay, churn). The root split is our split A at the midpoint 10.5. The
second split, `charge <= 87.50`, exists only to isolate *one* customer: the 6-month customer paying 90 who stayed.
Would you bet that customers paying over 87.5 never churn? Ten rows cannot support that rule. A tree will keep
splitting until every leaf is pure unless you stop it, which is the subject of the
[next lesson](/foundations/f8/growing-and-pruning-trees/).

:::note[Regression trees]
For a numeric target the recipe is the same with a different impurity: the **variance** (mean squared error around
the node's mean). The best split is the one that most reduces the weighted squared error, and each leaf predicts the
mean of its rows. `DecisionTreeRegressor` does this, and gradient boosting (lesson 4) is built from such trees.
:::

## Summary

- A decision tree is a flowchart of single-feature threshold questions; each leaf predicts the majority class (or
  the mean, for regression) of the training rows that reached it.
- Impurity measures how mixed a node is: Gini $= 1 - \sum_k p_k^2$ or entropy $= -\sum_k p_k \log_2 p_k$. Both are 0
  for a pure node.
- A split is scored by the size-weighted impurity of its children. The decrease in entropy is the information gain.
- Training is greedy: try every feature and every midpoint threshold, keep the best, recurse.
- Trees need no feature scaling and are easy to read, but they will happily memorise tiny groups of rows.
