ML runs on data about people: purchases, messages, medical records, photos, locations. Privacy asks what
someone could learn about a **particular person** from your dataset, your published statistics or your
trained model. The answer is usually "more than you would expect".

**Personal data** is any information relating to an identifiable person. That covers obvious identifiers
(name, email, ID number) but also anything that can single someone out when combined with other data:
a precise location history, a face, a device ID.

## Removing names is not anonymisation

The intuitive fix is to delete names and IDs and publish the rest. Three well-known cases show why that
fails:

- **Medical records and a governor (1990s).** Massachusetts released "anonymised" hospital records of
  state employees, keeping ZIP code, birth date and sex. Latanya Sweeney bought the voter roll for
  Cambridge, Massachusetts, which listed the same three fields with names, and matched them to identify
  the governor's own medical records. In later work she estimated that about 87% of the US population
  (using 1990 census data) was likely to be uniquely identified by **5-digit ZIP code, sex and date of
  birth** alone. Later estimates with newer census data were lower (about 63%), still a majority.
- **The Netflix Prize (data released 2006).** Netflix published movie ratings from about half a million
  subscribers with names removed. [Narayanan and Shmatikov](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0610105) showed that
  knowing 8 of someone's ratings (2 of which could be completely wrong) with dates accurate to within
  14 days was enough to uniquely identify 99% of records. Public reviews on other sites could supply that
  knowledge.
- **AOL search logs (2006).** AOL released three months of search queries with user names replaced by
  numbers. Journalists at the *New York Times* identified one user from the content of her searches alone.

The columns that do the damage are <Term id="quasi-identifier" text="quasi-identifiers" />: not identifying
alone, but identifying in combination, and available elsewhere for linking.

:::caution[Rich data is identifying data]
The more columns a record has, and the more detailed each one is, the more likely it is unique. For
high-dimensional data like ratings, locations or browsing histories, almost every record is unique, and
"anonymised" is rarely a safe description.
:::

## k-anonymity

<Term id="k-anonymity" cap /> is a classic, simple test. A table is **k-anonymous** with respect to a set of
quasi-identifiers if every combination of their values that appears is shared by **at least $k$ rows**.
Then any record links to at least $k$ people, not one. To compute $k$, group by the quasi-identifiers and
take the smallest group size.

```python
import pandas as pd

df = pd.DataFrame({
    "age":       [34, 36, 31, 52, 57, 55, 38, 33],
    "postcode":  ["LS6 1AA", "LS6 2BB", "LS6 3CC", "YO1 7DD", "YO1 8EE", "YO1 9FF", "LS6 4GG", "LS6 5HH"],
    "diagnosis": ["flu", "asthma", "flu", "diabetes", "flu", "asthma", "diabetes", "flu"],
})
print(df.groupby(["age", "postcode"]).size().min())
```

```text title="Output"
1
```

$k = 1$: every person is unique on (age, postcode). The fix is **generalisation**: coarsen the
quasi-identifiers until groups are large enough. Ages become decades, full postcodes become districts:

```python
gen = pd.DataFrame({
    "age": (df["age"] // 10 * 10).astype(str) + "s",
    "area": df["postcode"].str.split().str[0],
    "diagnosis": df["diagnosis"],
})
print(gen.groupby(["age", "area"]).size())
```

```text title="Output"
age  area
30s  LS6     5
50s  YO1     3
dtype: int64
```

Now $k = 3$. The cost is detail: an analyst can no longer study age effects within a decade.

k-anonymity has well-known holes. If everyone in a group has the **same** diagnosis, knowing someone is in
the group reveals it (a **homogeneity attack**; refinements such as l-diversity require variety in the
sensitive column). And it only protects against linking on the columns you listed as quasi-identifiers.
These weaknesses led researchers to a stronger, mathematical definition.

## Differential privacy: the intuition

<Term id="differential-privacy" cap /> (DP) changes the question. Instead of making the **data** look
anonymous, it makes the **answers** computed from it barely depend on any single person. The promise:
whatever an attacker learns from the output, they would have learned almost the same if your record had
not been in the dataset at all. So joining the dataset carries little extra risk for you.

The standard way to achieve it is to add carefully sized random noise. For a **counting query** ("how
many patients have diabetes?"), adding or removing one person changes the true count by at most 1; this
is the query's **sensitivity**. The **Laplace mechanism** returns

$$
\text{noisy count} = \text{true count} + \text{Laplace}(0, b), \qquad b = \frac{\text{sensitivity}}{\varepsilon} = \frac{1}{\varepsilon}
$$

The Laplace distribution is a symmetric, peaked distribution centred on 0 with scale $b$; its standard
deviation is $\sqrt{2}\,b$. $\varepsilon$ (epsilon) is the **privacy parameter**: smaller $\varepsilon$
means more noise and stronger privacy. NumPy draws Laplace noise with `rng.laplace(0, b)`:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
true_count = 42
for eps in [1.0, 0.1]:
    noisy = true_count + rng.laplace(0, 1 / eps, size=5)
    print(eps, noisy.round(1))
```

```text title="Output"
1.0 [42.3 41.4 39.5 38.6 43. ]
0.1 [59.5 44.4 48.1 42.9 62.4]
```

With $\varepsilon = 1$, answers are within a few of 42: useful for a count in the thousands, and no single
person changes the picture. With $\varepsilon = 0.1$, the noise is ten times larger.

Three things to know about DP:

- **Privacy loss adds up.** Asking many questions spends privacy: roughly, $k$ queries at $\varepsilon$
  each cost $k\varepsilon$ in total. Systems track a **privacy budget**. Repeating one query and averaging
  would otherwise cancel the noise.
- **It is used in practice.** The US Census Bureau used differential privacy to protect 2020 census
  results, and several technology companies use it to collect usage statistics.
- **It applies to training too.** **DP-SGD** clips each example's gradient and adds noise during
  stochastic gradient descent (F6), so the trained model provably depends little on any one example, at
  some cost in accuracy.

:::note[Why noise and not rounding?]
Any deterministic answer can, in principle, be compared across two datasets that differ by one person.
Randomness is what makes the outputs on "with you" and "without you" nearly indistinguishable. The
mathematical definition says the probability of any output changes by at most a factor $e^{\varepsilon}$
when one person is added or removed.
:::

## Models remember: memorisation

A trained model is a summary of its data, and sometimes too good a summary. Large models with many
parameters can **memorise** rare training examples verbatim. In 2021, [Carlini and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07805)
showed that querying GPT-2 could extract hundreds of verbatim sequences from its training data, including
names, phone numbers and email addresses that appeared in just one document, and found larger models
more vulnerable than smaller ones. Similar extraction has since been shown for image generation models.

Related attacks:

- **Membership inference**: guess whether a particular record was in the training set, often from how
  confident the model is on it (models tend to be more confident on examples they trained on).
- **Model inversion**: reconstruct typical features of a class or person from a model's outputs.

This applies directly to the large language models from F10. The takeaway: if sensitive data goes in, assume a
model **might** be able to give some of it back.

## Data minimisation and other defences

The cheapest privacy protection is data you never collected. **Data minimisation** means collecting and
keeping only what the stated purpose needs, for only as long as it needs it. Practical habits:

1. Drop columns that the model does not need before they reach the training pipeline.
2. Coarsen what you keep: year of birth instead of date, district instead of full address.
3. Delete raw data on a schedule; keep aggregates if they suffice.
4. Remove duplicates and obvious personal identifiers (emails, phone numbers) from text corpora, since
   repeated data is memorised more readily.
5. Restrict who can access sensitive columns, including the group attribute kept for fairness audits.
6. Where the risk justifies the accuracy cost, train or release statistics with differential privacy.

Data protection law in many places, such as the EU's GDPR, builds in principles like purpose limitation
and data minimisation; the details are beyond this course.

## Summary

- Removing names is not anonymisation. Quasi-identifiers such as ZIP code, sex and birth date, or a few
  movie ratings, can single people out by linking with other data.
- A table is k-anonymous if every quasi-identifier combination appears at least $k$ times; generalising
  columns raises $k$ at the cost of detail, and homogeneous groups still leak.
- Differential privacy adds noise so outputs barely depend on any one person. The Laplace mechanism for a
  count adds noise of scale $1/\varepsilon$; smaller $\varepsilon$ means stronger privacy and noisier
  answers, and repeated queries spend the budget.
- Large models can memorise and regurgitate training data. Data minimisation, deduplication and DP
  training are the main defences.
