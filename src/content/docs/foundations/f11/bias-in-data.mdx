A model has no opinions. It finds whatever patterns in the training data best predict the labels. So
when people say a model is "biased", they nearly always mean that the **data** carried a pattern we do
not want repeated, and the model faithfully learned it. <Term id="algorithmic-bias" /> is mostly
data bias plus an optimiser that does its job.

This lesson names the usual sources, then builds a small example in pandas where a model that never sees
group membership still treats the groups very differently.

## Where bias enters

Think of the path from the world to a model: something happens, someone records and labels it,
a dataset is assembled, a model is fitted. Each step can tilt things.

| Kind | What goes wrong | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Historical bias** | The world itself, as recorded, reflects past unfairness. Even perfect sampling copies it. | Ten years of hiring decisions in a field that mostly hired men. |
| **Sampling (representation) bias** | Some groups are under-represented, or the sample is not the population the model will serve. | Face datasets that are mostly lighter-skinned; a medical model trained at one hospital. |
| **Label bias** | The label is a flawed stand-in for what you care about, and the flaw differs by group. | "Arrested" used as a label for "committed a crime"; "health costs" used for "health need". |
| **Measurement bias** | Features are measured with different accuracy or meaning across groups. | A pulse oximeter or camera that reads less accurately on darker skin; a test score in a second language. |

Two more ideas complete the picture:

- **Selection effects from past decisions.** A lender only sees repayment outcomes for people it
  approved. If a group was rarely approved, the data say little about how they would have repaid. This is
  the feedback loop from the last lesson in another form.
- **Aggregation.** One model for everyone can fit the majority well and the minority badly when the
  relationship between features and label differs between groups. More data for the small group, or
  checking per-group error, is the fix.

:::note[Bias here is not the statistics word]
In statistics (F5), an estimator is *unbiased* when it is right on average, and its bias is its average error. In this module "bias" means a systematic
difference in how a system treats groups of people. Same word, different idea; context tells you which.
:::

## Proxies: why "just delete the column" fails

The first idea most people have is **fairness through unawareness**: remove the sensitive attribute
(gender, ethnicity, age) from the features. It rarely works, because other features carry the same
information. A <Term id="proxy-variable" /> is a feature that is correlated with the sensitive attribute:
postcode with ethnicity, first name or hobbies with gender, years of experience with age. Given enough
features, a model can reconstruct group membership quite well without ever being shown it. That is what
happened with the CV words in the last lesson.

You can measure this directly: try to **predict the sensitive attribute from the other features**. If
you can, so can your model.

:::caution[Keep the sensitive column for auditing]
Deleting the sensitive attribute also deletes your ability to *check* fairness, because you can no
longer compute metrics per group. Many teams keep it, under access controls, for evaluation only, even
when the model may not use it as an input. Whether you are allowed to collect and use it depends on the
context and on law, which varies by country.
:::

## Watching a model learn bias

Here is a synthetic hiring history. Skill has **the same distribution** in groups A and B. Where people
live is strongly tied to group. And past decision makers held group B to a higher bar:

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n = 4000
group = rng.choice(["A", "B"], size=n)
# Where people live is tied to group: a proxy.
area = np.where(group == "A",
                rng.choice(["north", "south"], size=n, p=[0.8, 0.2]),
                rng.choice(["north", "south"], size=n, p=[0.2, 0.8]))
skill = rng.normal(0, 1, size=n)                     # same distribution in both groups
# Past decisions: group B needed a higher skill score to be hired.
hired = skill > np.where(group == "A", 0.0, 0.8)

hist = pd.DataFrame({"group": group, "area": area,
                     "skill_band": pd.cut(skill, [-np.inf, 0, 0.8, np.inf], labels=["low", "mid", "high"]),
                     "hired": hired})
print(hist.groupby("group")["hired"].mean().round(2))
```

```text title="Output"
group
A    0.48
B    0.20
Name: hired, dtype: float64
```

Now "train" the simplest possible model that is **blind to group**: for each combination of area and
skill band, predict "hire" if at least half of such people were hired in the past. This lookup table is
exactly what a flexible classifier would learn from these two features, given enough data.

```python
table = hist.groupby(["area", "skill_band"], observed=True)["hired"].mean()
print(table.round(2))
rate = table.loc[list(zip(hist["area"], hist["skill_band"]))].to_numpy()
hist["pred"] = rate >= 0.5

mid = hist[hist["skill_band"] == "mid"]          # people with the same, middling skill
print(mid.groupby("group")["pred"].mean().round(2))
```

```text title="Output"
area   skill_band
north  low           0.00
       mid           0.81
       high          1.00
south  low           0.00
       mid           0.17
       high          1.00
Name: hired, dtype: float64
group
A    0.82
B    0.18
Name: pred, dtype: float64
```

Read the table: in the mid band, northerners were usually hired and southerners usually were not,
because most northerners are in group A. The model never saw the `group` column, yet among people with
**the same skill band**, it recommends 82% of group A and 18% of group B. Area is doing the work of
group. The model is accurate at reproducing history, and that is exactly the problem.

:::tip[Accuracy against biased labels rewards bias]
If the labels are past human decisions, a model that copies those decisions scores well. A fairer model
might score *worse* on the same labels. Before trusting a high accuracy, ask what the labels measure.
:::

## How models absorb bias, in general

The example used a lookup table, but every model you met in F7 to F10 does a version of the same
thing. Training minimises average loss over the training data (F6). That has three consequences:

1. **Patterns in the labels are learned, wanted or not.** Including those that run through proxies.
2. **The majority dominates the average.** If 90% of examples come from one group, errors on the other
   10% barely move the loss, so the model may fit them poorly. This is how sampling bias turns into
   higher error for under-represented groups.
3. **Models can amplify.** A classifier that thresholds a score can turn a modest difference in the data
   into a large difference in decisions, as the 82% versus 18% above shows.

What helps, roughly in order of how often it is the real fix:

- **Better data**: collect more from under-represented groups, audit how labels were made, choose a target
  that measures what you actually care about.
- **Measure per group** before and after every change (next lesson).
- **Mitigations** inside or after the model, such as reweighting examples or adjusting thresholds per
  group, with their trade-offs (lesson 4).

## Summary

- Bias usually enters through data: historical bias, sampling bias, label bias and measurement bias, plus
  selection effects from past decisions.
- Removing a sensitive attribute does not stop a model using it, because proxies such as postcode carry the
  same information. Keep the attribute (carefully) so you can audit.
- A group-blind model trained on biased decisions reproduced them: same skill band, 82% versus 18%.
- Training on average loss lets the majority dominate and can amplify small differences into large ones.
