So far this course has been about making models *work*: arrays, gradients, probability, optimisation, and
in F7 to F10 classifiers, trees, neural networks and language models. This module asks a different question:
**work for whom, and at what cost?** A model that is 95% accurate overall can still be wrong in a
systematic way for one group of people, leak the private data it was trained on, or break silently when
the world changes. None of these show up in a single accuracy number.

The point of this module is not to make you afraid of building things. It is to give you the habits of
an engineer who checks: measure per group, ask where the labels came from, keep less data than you could,
and write down what your model is not for.

## Three documented cases

These are real, widely reported cases. The details matter, so each one says what was actually found and
by whom.

### A hiring tool that learned from past hiring

In 2018, [Reuters reported](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-amazon-com-jobs-automation-insight-idUSKCN1MK08G)
that Amazon had built an experimental tool to rate job applicants' CVs. It was trained on CVs submitted
over the previous ten years, most of which came from men, reflecting who worked in tech. According to
the report, the system learned to penalise CVs containing the word "women's" (as in "women's chess club
captain") and downgraded graduates of two all-women's colleges. Amazon edited the system to be neutral
to those particular terms, but could not be sure it would not find other ways to sort candidates
unfairly, and the team was disbanded. Amazon said the tool was never used by its recruiters to evaluate
candidates.

The lesson: nobody told the model to prefer men. It was asked to imitate past decisions, and past
decisions were skewed. Removing the obvious word does not remove the pattern, because many other words
correlate with it. Lesson 2 calls these **proxies**.

### Face analysis that works better for some faces

In the 2018 [Gender Shades](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v81/buolamwini18a.html) study, Joy Buolamwini
and Timnit Gebru tested three commercial systems that classify the gender of a face. They built a test
set balanced by skin type and gender, and found that darker-skinned women were the most misclassified
group, with error rates of up to 34.7%, while the maximum error rate for lighter-skinned men was 0.8%.
Popular benchmark datasets of the time were mostly lighter-skinned faces, so headline accuracy hid the
gap.

A larger government study followed. In December 2019 the US National Institute of Standards and
Technology (NIST) [evaluated 189 face recognition algorithms](https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2019/12/nist-study-evaluates-effects-race-age-sex-face-recognition-software)
from 99 developers. For one-to-one matching (is this the same person?), many algorithms had false
positive rates 10 to 100 times higher for some demographic groups than for others, depending on the
algorithm. NIST also stressed that algorithms varied widely: some showed much smaller differences, so
the gap is a property of particular systems and their training data, not an unavoidable law.

### A health algorithm that predicted the wrong thing

A 2019 paper in *Science* by [Obermeyer and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aax2342) studied
a widely used commercial algorithm that US health systems used to pick patients for extra care
programmes. It predicted each patient's future **health-care costs**, as a stand-in for their health
**needs**. Because of unequal access to care, less money was spent on Black patients than on equally
sick White patients. So at the same risk score, Black patients were considerably sicker. The authors
estimated that fixing the disparity would raise the share of Black patients selected for extra help from
17.7% to 46.5%.

The lesson: the model may have predicted costs well. The harm came from **choosing the target**. "What
exactly are we predicting, and is it the thing we care about?" is a fairness question, not just a
modelling one.

:::note[Allocation and quality of service]
Two kinds of harm recur. **Allocation** harms decide who gets a job interview, a loan, extra care or
extra police attention. **Quality of service** harms mean the product simply works worse for some
people, like a face matcher or a speech recogniser that makes more errors for some accents. Both are
worth measuring; they call for different metrics.
:::

## Feedback loops: when predictions make their own data

Some systems change the world they later learn from. Predictive policing is the standard example. If
officers are sent where past records show the most crime, they find more incidents *there*, simply
because that is where they are looking. Those incidents enter the records, which send even more patrols
the same way. Researchers have analysed this both with real data (Lum and Isaac, 2016, using drug
arrest records from Oakland) and mathematically ([Ensign and colleagues, 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.09847),
"Runaway feedback loops in predictive policing").

A <Term id="feedback-loop" /> is easy to simulate. Two districts have **exactly the same** true rate of
incidents. District 0 starts with one extra incident on record. Each day the single patrol goes to
whichever district has more records, and incidents only get recorded where the patrol is:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
true_rate = np.array([0.3, 0.3])   # the same real chance of finding an incident in both districts
recorded = np.array([6, 5])        # district 0 starts with one extra incident on record

for day in range(365):
    d = np.argmax(recorded)                       # patrol where the records say crime is
    recorded[d] += rng.random() < true_rate[d]    # incidents are only recorded where you look

print(recorded)
print("share of records in district 0:", round(recorded[0] / recorded.sum(), 2))
```

```text title="Output"
[101   5]
share of records in district 0: 0.95
```

After a year, 95% of the records point at district 0, and the data now "prove" it is the high-crime
district. The data are not lying about what was recorded; they are silent about what was never looked
for. The same shape appears in recommendation systems (you only get feedback on items you showed), in
lending (you only learn whether *approved* applicants repay), and in content moderation.

:::caution[A model's training data are often its own past decisions]
Whenever a model decides what gets observed, its future training data are filtered by its past
predictions. Checking for this before deployment, and keeping some decisions independent of the model
(for example a small amount of random exploration), are standard defences.
:::

## What "responsible AI" covers

The rest of the module takes the main concerns one at a time:

| Lesson | Question |
|---|---|
| Bias in data and models | Where does unfairness come from, if nobody programs it in? |
| Measuring fairness | How do you put numbers on "the model treats groups differently"? |
| Calibration and trade-offs | Why can't one model satisfy every fairness definition at once? |
| Privacy | How can training data leak, and how do you protect people in a dataset? |
| Safety, reliability and transparency | What happens when the world changes, and how do you document and oversee a model? |

Three principles run through all of it:

1. **Disaggregate.** Report metrics per group, not just overall. Averages hide the people the system fails.
2. **Question the data.** Who collected it, who is missing, and what do the labels really measure?
3. **Keep humans accountable.** A model is a tool someone chose to deploy. "The algorithm decided" is not
   an answer anyone affected should accept.

:::tip[Not only an ethics problem]
Every case above is also an engineering failure: wrong target, unrepresentative test set, unmeasured
subgroup errors, feedback in the data. The fixes are mostly engineering too, which is why they belong in
an ML course rather than a separate one.
:::

## Summary

- Reuters reported that an experimental hiring tool learned to penalise CVs mentioning "women's" from ten
  years of mostly male CVs; removing the word did not guarantee removing the bias.
- Gender Shades found error rates of up to 34.7% for darker-skinned women versus at most 0.8% for
  lighter-skinned men in commercial gender classifiers; NIST found large demographic differences in many,
  but not all, face recognition algorithms.
- A health algorithm used cost as a proxy for need, and so under-selected Black patients who were just as
  sick.
- Feedback loops arise when a model's decisions control what data are collected; even identical districts
  can end up looking completely different.
- Harms are of allocation (who gets something) or quality of service (who it works for). Always report
  metrics per group.
