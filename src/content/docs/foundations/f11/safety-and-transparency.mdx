The earlier lessons asked whether a model is fair and whether it protects the people in its data. This
one asks whether it **keeps working** once deployed, whether people can tell **what it is for and where
it fails**, and what can go wrong when capable systems pursue goals we specified imperfectly.

## Robustness: small changes, big mistakes

Models can be surprisingly fragile:

- **Adversarial examples.** In image classifiers, a tiny, carefully computed change to the pixels,
  invisible to a person, can flip the prediction. The perturbation is found by following the gradient
  of the loss with respect to the **input** (the same gradient machinery as F4 and F6, pointed at the
  image instead of the weights).
- **Natural perturbations.** Blur, lighting, a different camera, a typo, an unusual accent: inputs that
  are perfectly ordinary but unlike most of the training data.
- **Prompt injection** in language-model systems: instructions hidden in a web page or document that the
  model follows as if they came from the user. Track C covers this in depth.

## Distribution shift

Training assumes the future looks like the past: that test and deployment data come from the same
distribution as training data. <Term id="distribution-shift" /> is when that stops being true. Common
forms:

| Kind | What changes | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Covariate shift** | the inputs $x$ | a new camera model, a different hospital's scanner, a sensor that drifts |
| **Label (prior) shift** | the base rate of $y$ | fraud is rare in training but spikes during an attack |
| **Concept shift** | the relationship between $x$ and $y$ | what counts as spam changes as spammers adapt |

A tiny simulation. A one-feature classifier learns the best threshold on clean data, and is then deployed
where the sensor reads high by an offset:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
def sample(n, offset=0.0):
    y = rng.random(n) < 0.5
    x = rng.normal(np.where(y, 2.0, 0.0), 1.0) + offset   # offset = a sensor that reads high
    return x, y

x_train, y_train = sample(5000)
thresholds = np.linspace(-2, 4, 601)
acc = [np.mean((x_train > t) == y_train) for t in thresholds]
t_best = thresholds[np.argmax(acc)]
print("threshold:", round(t_best, 2))

for offset in [0.0, 1.0, 2.0]:
    x, y = sample(5000, offset)
    print("offset", offset, "accuracy", round(np.mean((x > t_best) == y), 3))
```

```text title="Output"
threshold: 1.0
offset 0.0 accuracy 0.843
offset 1.0 accuracy 0.732
offset 2.0 accuracy 0.582
```

Nothing about the model changed, and it raised no error. Accuracy just fell from 84% to 58%, close to a
coin flip. That is why deployed models need **monitoring**: track input statistics and, when labels
arrive, accuracy over time (Track A covers monitoring and drift detection). Shift also interacts with
fairness: if one group's data drift more (a new branch opens in their area), their error rates rise first.

### Shortcut learning

A model can score well for the wrong reason, by latching onto a feature that happens to predict the
label in the training data. A 2018 study by Zech and colleagues (*PLOS Medicine*) found that a
pneumonia detector trained on chest X-rays could also identify which hospital system an image came from,
and that its performance was often worse on hospitals it had not been trained on: hospital differences in
equipment and patient mix were part of what it had learned. Shortcuts look like success on a test set
drawn from the same source, and fail the moment the source changes. Testing on data from a **different**
source than training is the best way to catch them.

## Transparency: model cards and datasheets

If a model will be used by people who did not build it, they need to know what it is for and where it
fails. Two widely adopted documents:

**<Term id="model-card" text="Model cards" />** ([Mitchell and colleagues, 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.03993))
are short documents shipped with a trained model. The paper proposes sections including:

- model details (who built it, version, type);
- **intended use**, and uses that are out of scope;
- factors (groups, environments) the performance may vary across;
- metrics, with evaluation data described;
- **quantitative results broken down by group**, exactly the disaggregated metrics from lesson 3;
- ethical considerations, caveats and recommendations.

**Datasheets for datasets** ([Gebru and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09010)) do the same for
data, by analogy with the datasheets that come with electronic components: why the dataset was created,
what it contains, how it was collected and labelled, who is (and is not) represented, what
pre-processing was done, recommended and inappropriate uses, and how it is maintained.

Model hubs such as Hugging Face show a model card on every model page, so you will read many of these. In
the homework you will write a short version for your own audit.

:::tip[Explanations]
Transparency also covers explaining individual predictions: which features pushed this applicant's score
down? Tools for this (feature importance, SHAP, counterfactual explanations) are covered in Tracks A and B.
A simple model you can read is often better than a complex one plus an explanation tool.
:::

## Human oversight

"Keep a human in the loop" is common advice, and it only works if the human can actually overrule the
model. Two failure modes:

- **Automation bias**: people tend to accept a system's output, especially under time pressure, so the
  human reviewer becomes a rubber stamp.
- **No route to challenge**: people affected by a decision cannot find out it was automated, or cannot
  appeal it.

Meaningful oversight means reviewers who have the time, information and authority to disagree; logging
decisions so they can be audited; and a way for affected people to contest outcomes.

## Misuse and dual use

Some harms come not from errors but from the system working as built, in the wrong hands: synthetic
media used to impersonate real people, face recognition used for covert mass surveillance, language
models used to produce spam or scams at scale. Responsible release practices include assessing misuse
before launch, staged access, usage policies and monitoring, and declining to build or release some
capabilities at all.

## AI safety and alignment, briefly

As systems become more capable and are given more autonomy (acting in software, writing and running code,
browsing the web), a further set of concerns grows in importance. The 2016 paper [Concrete Problems in AI
Safety](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.06565) named several, which remain central:

- **Specification gaming (reward hacking).** An optimiser finds a way to score highly on the objective you
  wrote down without doing what you meant. A widely cited example is an agent trained on a boat-racing
  game that learned to circle endlessly collecting points instead of finishing the race. Optimisation
  (F6) is very good at exploiting gaps between the objective and the intent.
- **Side effects.** Achieving the goal while breaking things nobody thought to forbid.
- **Safe exploration and robustness to shift**, as above.
- **Scalable oversight.** How do you supervise a system on tasks where checking its work is hard for you?

**Alignment** is the research area concerned with making AI systems pursue the goals their designers
and users actually intend, and stay honest and controllable as they get more capable. Experts disagree
about how quickly risks will grow and which are most serious, but the basic engineering point is not
controversial: the more autonomy and capability a system has, the more a mis-specified objective
matters. Track B and Track C return to evaluation and safety techniques for large models.

## Regulation (as of late 2026)

Governments have begun regulating AI. The most comprehensive law so far is the **EU AI Act**
([Regulation (EU) 2024/1689](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)), which entered into force in
August 2024 with obligations phasing in over several years. It takes a **risk-based approach**: a few uses
are prohibited, "high-risk" uses (examples in its annexes include some uses in hiring, credit and access
to essential services) face requirements such as risk management, data governance, documentation, human
oversight and accuracy and robustness, and other uses carry lighter transparency duties. Timelines and
guidance have been revised since adoption, and other countries take different approaches. This is not
legal advice: if you build systems in a regulated area, read the current official texts and work with
people who know the law.

Notice how much of this lesson maps onto what such rules ask for: documented data (datasheets),
documented models (model cards), per-group evaluation (lesson 3), robustness testing and monitoring, and
human oversight. Good engineering practice and compliance point the same way.

## Summary

- Models can fail on tiny adversarial changes, on ordinary but unfamiliar inputs, and silently under
  distribution shift: a sensor offset took the example from 84% to 58% accuracy with no error raised.
- Shortcut learning makes a model look good on same-source test data; test on data from a different source.
- Model cards document intended use and per-group performance; datasheets document how a dataset was made
  and who it covers.
- Human oversight needs reviewers who can really disagree, plus logging and a route to contest decisions.
- Safety concerns include misuse and specification gaming; alignment research aims to keep capable systems
  pursuing intended goals.
- As of late 2026 the EU AI Act regulates AI by risk level, with obligations phasing in; check the official
  text for anything that matters.
