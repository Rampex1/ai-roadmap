Getting to mastery in AI takes hundreds of hours. How you spend those hours matters as much as how many you spend.
This lesson covers the few study habits that research supports most strongly, and shows how this site builds them in,
so you can trust the system and spend your effort where it counts.

## Three ideas from learning science

### 1. Retrieval practice: test yourself, don't reread

The most natural way to study is to reread your notes until they feel familiar. It is also one of the least effective.
Familiarity is a trap: the page *looks* known, so it feels learned, but you have not practised the thing you will actually
need to do later, which is pulling the idea out of your own head.

<Term id="retrieval-practice" cap /> means answering questions from memory. Each time you successfully recall something,
the memory gets stronger, and even a *failed* attempt followed by the correct answer helps more than rereading.
Psychologists call this the testing effect, and it has been replicated many times.

That is why every lesson here ends with a quiz, and why the quiz tells you to answer from memory first.
Scrolling back up to find the answer turns a memory workout into a reading exercise.

:::tip[A two-second rule]
Before you click an option, say your answer to yourself, or write it down. Committing to an answer, even a wrong one,
is what makes the feedback stick.
:::

### 2. Spacing: review just before you forget

You forget most new facts within days. This decay is called the forgetting curve. The good news is that each review,
if it comes at the right moment, makes the next decline slower. So the gaps between reviews can grow: a day, then a few
days, then weeks, then months.

That is <Term id="spaced-repetition" />. Doing your reviews in spaced sessions beats cramming the same amount into one
session, even though cramming feels more productive at the time.

### 3. Interleaving: mix it up

If you practise ten questions on topic A and then ten on topic B, each question tells you in advance which idea to use.
Mix A, B and C together and you must first work out *which* idea a question needs, which is exactly the skill a real problem
demands. This is <Term id="interleaving" />. It feels harder, and it usually works better.

:::note[Desirable difficulty]
All three techniques feel harder than rereading. That effort is the point: researchers call it a *desirable difficulty*.
If studying feels easy, check that you are actually retrieving, not just recognising.
:::

## How this site uses them

### Lesson quizzes

Each lesson has a pool of at least eight questions. Every time you load the page you see **five, picked at random**,
so a second visit gives you a partly new set. Question types include multiple choice, "select all that apply",
numbers, "what does this code print?", putting steps in order, and fill-in-the-blank.

After you answer, you see why each option is right or wrong. Read the explanation even when you were right:
sometimes you were right for the wrong reason.

### Review: your daily spaced practice

The first time you answer a question, the site creates a review card for it. From then on the **Review** page
(in the top bar) shows the cards that are due today: up to 50 per session, mixed across modules.

The schedule comes from **FSRS** (Free Spaced Repetition Scheduler), an open-source algorithm. In plain words, for every
card FSRS keeps track of:

- **Retrievability**: its estimate of the chance you could recall the answer *right now*. This drops as time passes.
- **Stability**: how slowly that chance drops. Precisely, it is the number of days it takes to fall from 100% to 90%.
  Each successful review raises stability, so the card comes back less often.
- **Difficulty**: how hard this particular card is for you, which controls how much stability grows after each review.

FSRS schedules the next review for roughly when your chance of remembering falls to **90%**. Two rules on this site
keep the data honest:

- **Right means Good, wrong means Again.** There is no "hard" or "easy" button. A wrong answer brings the card back soon.
- **Only your first answer to a question each day counts.** You can retry for practice, but the schedule only uses the
  first attempt. Otherwise you could "pass" a card just by remembering what you clicked a minute ago.

Exercises (the code boxes) are *not* review cards. Only quiz questions are.

### Mastery checks and testing out

Each module ends with a <Term id="mastery-check" />: **12 questions** drawn from all of the module's lessons, interleaved,
and chosen to favour the questions you are weakest on. Score **80% or more** and the module is marked mastered.
If you score less, the result lists the lessons to revisit. A retake avoids the questions from your previous attempt
where it can, so you can't pass by memorising one set.

Already know a topic? The module page lets you take the same check **before** the lessons. That is called **testing out**.
Pass it and you can skip ahead with a clear conscience. Either way, your answers feed your review cards.

### Your progress lives in this browser

There are no accounts. Your progress is saved in this browser on this computer, so:

- clearing your browser's site data, or switching browsers, starts you from zero;
- **Settings** has an **Export progress** button that downloads a backup file. Export once a week (the dashboard reminds
  you), and use **Import** to restore it.

## A study routine you can keep

Consistency beats intensity. Four 45-minute sessions a week, every week, will take you further than one exhausting
weekend a month. Here is a pattern that works for most people:

1. **Review first (5–15 minutes).** Open Review and clear what is due. Starting with review warms up your memory and keeps
   the pile from growing.
2. **One new lesson (15–30 minutes).** Read actively: run every command, try every exercise *before* opening a hint,
   then take the quiz from memory.
3. **Stop while you still have energy.** Note where you will start next time.

A useful rule for the week: **do not skip review two days in a row.** Missing one day is fine; FSRS adapts. But reviews pile
up, and a pile of 200 cards is how people quit.

### Worked example: how long will Foundations take?

Suppose you study 5 days a week for 45 minutes a day. That is 5 × 45 = 225 minutes, or 3.75 hours a week.
Foundations has roughly 90 lessons. If each lesson, with its quiz and exercises, takes about 25 minutes, plus a share of
homework and review, budget about 40 minutes per lesson in total: 90 × 40 = 3,600 minutes, which is 60 hours.
At 3.75 hours a week that is 60 ÷ 3.75 = **16 weeks**, about four months. Slower is fine. Steady matters more than fast.

## Using the AI tutor well

Every lesson has an **Ask the tutor** button, and quiz feedback and code errors have an **Explain this with the tutor**
button. The tutor is **optional and off by default**; you connect it in **Settings** to a free model, either online
(for example OpenRouter's free models) or running on your own Mac (Ollama). Nothing else on the site needs it.

The tutor already knows which lesson you are on and is told to give hints before full solutions. To learn from it rather
than outsource your thinking:

- **Try first.** Ask after you have made an attempt, and say what you tried: "I expected 5 but got 4.5 because…".
- **Ask for a hint, not the answer.** "Give me one hint" keeps the retrieval work with you.
- **Explain it back.** After an explanation, close the drawer and explain the idea in your own words, or ask the tutor
  to quiz you on it.
- **Stay sceptical.** Language models can be confidently wrong, and small free models more so. If the tutor disagrees
  with the lesson, check the lesson's Go deeper source.
- **Mind your privacy.** Some free tiers let the provider use your prompts (the Settings page says which). Never paste
  passwords or API keys into a chat.

## Summary

- Retrieval, spacing and interleaving are effortful, and that effort is what makes learning last.
- Lesson quizzes show 5 random questions from a pool; answering creates review cards that FSRS schedules for when your
  recall would drop to about 90%.
- Mastery checks are 12 mixed questions with an 80% pass mark; taken before the lessons, the same check lets you test out.
- Back up your progress from Settings, and study little and often: review first, then one new lesson.
