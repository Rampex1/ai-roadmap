[F10](/foundations/f10/llms-in-practice/) listed the ways LLMs fail. An engineer needs two more things:
the **mechanism** behind each failure, so you can predict when it will bite, and the **mitigation**, so
you can design around it. The rest of Track C is largely a catalogue of mitigations; this lesson maps
them out.

All the examples below are real, greedy outputs from `Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct`, a deliberately small model
that fails often enough to study. Large models fail less often, in the same ways.

## Hallucination: why it happens

Asked "Who wrote the 2019 novel The Glass Orchard? Answer in one sentence." (no such novel exists), the
model said:

```text title="Real output"
The Glass Orchard was written by the British author Jhumpa Lahiri.
```

Four mechanisms combine:

1. **The objective rewards plausibility.** Pretraining maximises the probability of real text. "X was
   written by [a well-known author]" is exactly the shape of a correct answer, so the model produces it.
2. **Rare facts are learned weakly.** Facts seen thousands of times in training (the capital of France)
   are recalled reliably; facts seen once or twice, or never, are blurred into "something plausible".
   Hallucination is most common on the long tail: obscure people, specific numbers, exact citations,
   recent events.
3. **Commitment snowballs.** Generation never goes back. The logprobs from
   [lesson 2](/llm/c1/decoding/) showed the model gave the first letter, " J", only 3.6% probability;
   but once chosen, "humpa Lahiri" followed with near certainty. Each token is conditioned to be
   *consistent with* the earlier ones, not to be *true*, so a single guess grows into a confident
   sentence.
4. **Training and evaluation reward guessing.** If benchmarks score a wrong answer and "I don't know"
   both as zero, a model tuned to score well learns to guess. A 2025 OpenAI paper, *Why Language Models
   Hallucinate*, argues this is a main reason hallucinations survive post-training, and that evaluations
   should give credit for appropriate abstention.

None of this involves intent, and none of it is fixed by asking the model to "be accurate". It is
reduced by giving the model the facts, letting it look them up, and checking what comes out.

## Arithmetic and spelling: tokenisation quirks

Asked "What is 4837 * 291? Answer with just the number.":

```text title="Real output"
129007
```

The answer is 1,407,567; the model is wrong by more than a factor of ten and has the wrong number of
digits. Two structural reasons, beyond the small model size:

- **The model writes the most significant digit first,** but multiplication's carries flow from the
  least significant digit. To get the first digit right, the model must effectively finish the whole
  calculation before writing anything, with no scratch paper.
- **Numbers are tokenised inconsistently.** The tokenisers from [lesson 1](/llm/c1/llm-as-a-function/)
  split the same number differently:

  ```text title="How ' 12345678' is tokenised"
  Qwen 2.5   ['Ġ', '1', '2', '3', '4', '5', '6', '7', '8']    one token per digit
  Gemma 3    ['▁', '1', '2', '3', '4', '5', '6', '7', '8']    one token per digit
  Llama 3.2  ['Ġ', '123', '456', '78']                           groups of up to three, from the left
  ```

  With left-to-right groups of three, the same digit position lands in different tokens depending on the
  number's length, so place value is harder to learn.

Spelling fails for the same reason [F10](/foundations/f10/tokens/) gave: the model sees tokens, not
letters. In Qwen's tokeniser "strawberry" is `['str', 'aw', 'berry']` at the start of a text but the
single token `' strawberry'` after a space, so the same word reaches the model in two unrelated forms.

Reasoning models, which write intermediate steps before answering, do much better on arithmetic because
the steps act as the scratch paper. But the engineering answer is simpler: **do not ask a language model
to be a calculator.** Give it a tool that runs `4837 * 291` in Python (C6), and use its strength,
deciding *what* to calculate.

## Knowledge cutoff: not knowing what you do not know

Asked "Who won the 2026 FIFA World Cup?", the 2024-era model said:

```text title="Real output"
The 2026 FIFA World Cup was won by the South Korean team, the South Korea national football team. The
team defeated the United States in the final, winning the tournament for the third time.
```

The tournament was after the model's <Term id="knowledge-cutoff" />, so this is invented, down to a
fictional "third time". The deeper problem is that nothing told the model what today's date is or that
its knowledge ended. Some templates now inject this: the Llama 3.2 template in lesson 1 adds "Cutting
Knowledge Date: December 2023" and today's date to every system prompt, which gives the model a chance
to say "that is after my training data".

Mitigations: put the current date in the system prompt; retrieve current information (search, your own
documents) into the context; and for anything time-sensitive, prefer a tool call over the model's memory.

## Sensitivity to phrasing and format

Small changes that should not matter can change the answer: the order of multiple-choice options, the
order of few-shot examples, "Answer:" versus "The answer is", a trailing space, or switching from
Markdown to JSON. Mechanically, each variant is a different token sequence, so it lands the model at a
different point in a space it learned from imperfect data, and any near tie between answers (lesson 2)
can flip.

Mitigations: evaluate on **many inputs and paraphrases**, not one impressive example (C9); fix the
prompt and its format once it passes; ask for **structured output** (a JSON schema the server enforces)
so parsing does not depend on wording (C3); and sample several times when the stakes justify it.

## The mitigation toolkit

| Failure | Mechanism | Mitigation | Where in Track C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hallucinated facts and citations | Plausibility objective, weak long-tail recall, snowballing | **Grounding**: retrieve sources into the prompt and require answers to cite them | C4 Embeddings, C5 RAG |
| Arithmetic, counting, dates, exact lookups | Tokens, single-pass computation | **Tools**: a calculator, code execution, a database or an API | C6 Tool use, C7 Agents, C8 MCP |
| Stale knowledge | Training data ends at the cutoff | Date in the prompt; retrieval and search tools | C3, C5, C6 |
| Phrasing sensitivity, format drift | Different token sequences, near ties | Structured output, fixed tested prompts, several samples | C3 Prompt and context engineering |
| Random errors on hard reasoning | Sampling noise, single-pass reasoning | Reasoning models; sample $n$ answers and vote | This lesson, C3 |
| "It looked fine in the demo" | One example is not evidence | **Evals**: test sets, automatic checks, LLM judges, regression runs | C9 Evals |
| Wrong style or format for a narrow task | General post-training | Fine-tuning on examples (for behaviour, not new facts) | C10 Fine-tuning |
| Prompt injection, unsafe output, cost blow-ups | Text instructions are just tokens | Guardrails, permissions, monitoring | C11 LLMOps, security and safety |

Two principles run through the table. **Move facts and computation out of the weights**: weights are a
lossy, frozen memory and a poor calculator, so retrieval and tools supply what they lack. And **measure,
do not hope**: every mitigation is itself unreliable, so an eval suite tells you whether it worked. The
Track C capstone combines both: a retrieval-augmented agent with tools served over MCP, checked by an
eval suite.

### Self-consistency: sample, then vote

For questions with a short, checkable answer, sampling several reasoning paths at moderate temperature and
taking the **most common final answer** is more accurate than a single greedy answer on many reasoning
benchmarks; this is *self-consistency* (Wang and colleagues, 2022). The agreement rate is also a cheap
confidence signal: if five samples give five different answers, the model is guessing, and the right
move is to **abstain** (say "I'm not sure", escalate to a person, or call a tool) rather than pick one.

The costs are real: $n$ samples cost $n$ times the tokens, and voting only works when answers can be
compared (a number, a label, a short string), not for free-form essays. The exercise implements the vote
with normalisation and an abstention threshold.

## Summary

- Hallucination comes from a plausibility objective, weakly learned long-tail facts, generation that
  commits token by token, and training that rewards guessing; ground answers in retrieved sources.
- Arithmetic and spelling fail because models see tokens, compute in one pass and write the most
  significant digit first; give them tools instead.
- Models do not know what happened after their cutoff, or even today's date, unless you tell them; put
  the date and fresh facts in the context.
- Phrasing and format sensitivity is real; test on many paraphrases, fix the prompt and use structured
  output.
- Move facts and computation out of the weights, sample and vote where answers are short and checkable,
  abstain when agreement is low, and measure everything with evals. C3 to C11 build these tools.
