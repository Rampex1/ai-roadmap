## Joint entropy, conditional entropy and mutual information

For two discrete random variables with joint distribution $p(x, y)$:

- **Joint entropy** $H(X, Y) = -\sum_{x, y} p(x, y)\log p(x, y)$: the uncertainty of the pair.
- **Conditional entropy** $H(X \mid Y) = -\sum_{x, y} p(x, y)\log p(x \mid y) = H(X, Y) - H(Y)$: the uncertainty left
  in $X$ once you know $Y$, averaged over $Y$. The second form is the **chain rule** for entropy.
- **Mutual information** is how much knowing one variable reduces uncertainty about the other:

$$
I(X; Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y) = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X, Y)
$$

The most useful form for research is a KL divergence, between the joint and the product of the marginals:

$$
I(X; Y) = D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(p(x, y) \,\|\, p(x)\,p(y)\big) = \sum_{x, y} p(x, y)\log\frac{p(x, y)}{p(x)\,p(y)}
$$

So <Term id="mutual-information" /> measures how far the pair is from being
[independent](/foundations/f5/conditional-probability/). It is symmetric, $I(X; Y) = I(Y; X)$; non-negative; and
zero **exactly** when $X$ and $Y$ are independent. Unlike correlation, it detects any kind of dependence, not just
linear.

A small example in bits: rain and umbrellas.

```python
import numpy as np

def H(p):
    p = np.asarray(p, dtype=float).ravel()
    p = p[p > 0]
    return -np.sum(p * np.log2(p))

P = np.array([[0.30, 0.10],     # rows: X = rain / no rain
              [0.10, 0.50]])    # columns: Y = umbrella / no umbrella
px, py = P.sum(axis=1), P.sum(axis=0)
print(px, py)
print(round(H(px), 4), round(H(py), 4), round(H(P), 4))
mi = H(px) + H(py) - H(P)
mi_kl = np.sum(P * np.log2(P / np.outer(px, py)))
print(round(mi, 4), round(mi_kl, 4), round(H(P) - H(py), 4))
print(round(H(np.outer(px, py)) - H(px) - H(py), 12))
```

```text title="Output"
[0.4 0.6] [0.4 0.6]
0.971 0.971 1.6855
0.2564 0.2564 0.7145
0.0
```

- The marginals come from summing rows and columns.
- Each variable alone carries 0.971 bits; the pair carries 1.6855, less than $2 \times 0.971$ because they overlap.
- The overlap, 0.2564 bits, is the mutual information, and the KL form gives the same number. $H(X \mid Y) = 0.7145$:
  knowing about the umbrella cuts uncertainty about rain from 0.971 to 0.7145 bits.
- For the independent table `np.outer(px, py)` the joint entropy is exactly the sum of the marginals: zero mutual
  information.

:::note[Mutual information in papers]
Contrastive representation learning (for example the InfoNCE loss) is motivated as maximising a lower bound on the
mutual information between two views of the same input. The information bottleneck trades off $I(X; Z)$ against
$I(Z; Y)$ for a representation $Z$. Estimating mutual information between continuous high-dimensional variables
is hard, which is why papers work with bounds rather than the quantity itself.
:::

## Why KL is never negative: Gibbs' inequality

F5 stated $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\|\, q) \ge 0$. The proof is Jensen's inequality for the concave $\log$
([lesson 3](/research/b0/convex-optimisation/)), $\mathbb{E}[\log Y] \le \log\mathbb{E}[Y]$, applied to
$Y = q(x)/p(x)$ with $x \sim p$:

$$
-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\|\, q) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\left[\log\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\right]
\le \log \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\left[\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\right]
= \log \sum_{x:\,p(x) > 0} q(x) \le \log 1 = 0
$$

For the KL to be zero, equality must hold in both inequalities. Jensen requires $q(x)/p(x)$ to be constant on
the support of $p$, and the final inequality requires $q$ to put all its mass there. Together these imply $q = p$. This argument
gives a family of results: $H(p, q) \ge H(p)$, $I(X; Y) \ge 0$ (it is a KL), and conditioning never increases
entropy on average, $H(X \mid Y) \le H(X)$.

Other properties worth knowing:

- **Not symmetric**, and it does not satisfy the triangle inequality, so it is not a metric.
- **Additive for independent pairs:** the KL between two product distributions is the sum of the KLs of the factors.
  That is why the KL between diagonal Gaussians is a sum over dimensions.
- **Closed form for Gaussians.** For one dimension, in nats,

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2) \,\|\, \mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)\big)
= \log\frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1} + \frac{\sigma_1^2 + (\mu_1 - \mu_2)^2}{2\sigma_2^2} - \frac{1}{2}
$$

and for $d$-dimensional Gaussians
$\tfrac{1}{2}\big[\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_2^{-1}\Sigma_1) + (\boldsymbol{\mu}_2 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_1)^\top\Sigma_2^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_2 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_1) - d + \log\frac{\det\Sigma_2}{\det\Sigma_1}\big]$.
The VAE loss uses this with $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, I)$ as the second Gaussian.

Because a KL is an expectation under $p$, it can also be estimated by [Monte Carlo](/research/b0/gaussians-and-monte-carlo/)
whenever you can sample from $p$ and evaluate both log-densities, which is how it is computed when there is no
closed form:

```python
from scipy import stats

def kl_gauss(m1, s1, m2, s2):
    """KL( N(m1, s1^2) || N(m2, s2^2) ) in nats."""
    return np.log(s2 / s1) + (s1**2 + (m1 - m2)**2) / (2 * s2**2) - 0.5

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=200_000)          # samples from p = N(0, 1)
log_ratio = stats.norm(0, 1).logpdf(x) - stats.norm(1, 2).logpdf(x)
print(round(kl_gauss(0, 1, 1, 2), 4), round(log_ratio.mean(), 4), "+/-", round(1.96 * log_ratio.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(x)), 4))
print(round(kl_gauss(1, 2, 0, 1), 4))
```

```text title="Output"
0.4431 0.4422 +/- 0.0026
1.3069
```

The Monte Carlo estimate agrees with the closed form within its error bar. Swapping the arguments gives 1.3069, a
different number: the asymmetry is large here.

### Forward and reverse KL

The asymmetry matters when you fit a simple model $q$ to a complicated $p$, because the two directions punish
different mistakes:

- **Forward KL**, $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\|\, q) = \mathbb{E}_p[\log p - \log q]$, averages over where $p$ has mass.
  If $q$ is tiny anywhere $p$ is not, the cost is huge. So $q$ spreads out to **cover every mode** of $p$, even if
  that puts mass where $p$ has none.
- **Reverse KL**, $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p) = \mathbb{E}_q[\log q - \log p]$, averages over where $q$ has mass.
  Putting mass where $p$ is tiny is what costs. So $q$ tends to **lock onto one mode** and ignore the others.

Fit a single Gaussian to a mixture of two bumps at $\pm 2$ (standard deviation 0.6), by grid search over its mean
and standard deviation, computing each KL by numerical integration:

```python
xs = np.linspace(-8, 8, 4001)
dx = xs[1] - xs[0]
p = 0.5 * stats.norm(-2, 0.6).pdf(xs) + 0.5 * stats.norm(2, 0.6).pdf(xs)   # two bumps

def kl_grid(a, b):
    m = a > 1e-300
    return np.sum(a[m] * (np.log(a[m]) - np.log(np.maximum(b[m], 1e-300)))) * dx

best_fwd = best_rev = (np.inf, None)
for m in np.linspace(-3, 3, 61):
    for s in np.linspace(0.3, 3, 28):
        q = stats.norm(m, s).pdf(xs)
        best_fwd = min(best_fwd, (kl_grid(p, q), (round(float(m), 2), round(float(s), 2))))
        best_rev = min(best_rev, (kl_grid(q, p), (round(float(m), 2), round(float(s), 2))))
print("forward KL(p||q) best (mean, sd):", best_fwd[1])
print("reverse KL(q||p) best (mean, sd):", best_rev[1])
```

```text title="Output"
forward KL(p||q) best (mean, sd): (0.0, 2.1)
reverse KL(q||p) best (mean, sd): (-2.0, 0.6)
```

Forward KL picks a broad Gaussian centred between the bumps, matching the mixture's mean and variance
(standard deviation $\sqrt{0.6^2 + 2^2} \approx 2.09$) and putting most of its mass in the valley where $p$ has little.
On this grid, reverse KL picks the parameters of one bump (the left one here; the right one is equally good).
Population maximum likelihood corresponds to forward KL; variational inference, including VAEs, minimises a reverse KL.

## Cross-entropy and maximum likelihood are the same thing

Let $p_{\text{data}}$ be the distribution the data come from and $q_\theta$ a model. Expand the forward KL:

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\text{data}} \,\|\, q_\theta) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}[-\log q_\theta(x)]}_{H(p_{\text{data}},\, q_\theta)} - H(p_{\text{data}})
$$

The entropy of the data does not depend on $\theta$, so minimising the KL over $\theta$ is the same as minimising
the cross-entropy. You cannot compute an expectation under $p_{\text{data}}$, but with $n$
<Term id="iid" text="i.i.d." /> samples you can estimate it by Monte Carlo:

$$
H(p_{\text{data}}, q_\theta) \approx -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log q_\theta(x_i)
$$

which is the average negative log-likelihood. Minimising it is
[maximum likelihood](/foundations/f5/maximum-likelihood/) on the observed sample:

> maximise likelihood = minimise average negative log-likelihood = minimise cross-entropy to the empirical
> distribution.

Minimising the population cross-entropy is equivalent to minimising the forward KL from the data distribution
to the model. The finite-sample objective estimates that population objective.

That is why maximum-likelihood models are "mode-covering": they are heavily penalised for giving near-zero
probability to anything that occurs in the data. A language model's expected per-token loss on the target distribution
is at least that distribution's per-token entropy under the same conditioning; the excess is the KL.

## The ELBO: a lower bound from Jensen

Latent-variable models explain data $x$ with unobserved $z$: $p_\theta(x) = \sum_z p_\theta(x, z)$ (or an integral).
For anything interesting this sum is intractable, so you cannot maximise $\log p_\theta(x)$ directly. The trick
that variational autoencoders (B9) rely on: pick **any** distribution $q(z)$ that is positive wherever
$p_\theta(x, z)$ is, and multiply and divide by it:

$$
\log p_\theta(x) = \log \sum_z q(z)\,\frac{p_\theta(x, z)}{q(z)} = \log \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q}\left[\frac{p_\theta(x, z)}{q(z)}\right]
\ge \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q}\left[\log\frac{p_\theta(x, z)}{q(z)}\right]
$$

The inequality is Jensen for the concave $\log$. The right-hand side is the
<Term id="elbo" text="evidence lower bound (ELBO)" />. It is an expectation under $q$, so it can be estimated by
sampling $z \sim q$, and it can be maximised by gradient ascent. How loose is it? A few lines of algebra (write
$p_\theta(x, z) = p_\theta(z \mid x)\,p_\theta(x)$) give the exact gap:

$$
\log p_\theta(x) - \text{ELBO}(q) = D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z) \,\|\, p_\theta(z \mid x)\big)
$$

So maximising the ELBO over $q$ pushes $q$ towards the true posterior (a reverse KL), and the bound is tight
exactly when $q$ is the posterior. Check it on a model with three latent values:

```python
prior = np.array([0.5, 0.3, 0.2])               # p(z) for z = 0, 1, 2
lik = np.array([0.10, 0.60, 0.30])              # p(x | z) for the one observed x
joint = prior * lik                             # p(x, z)
log_px = np.log(joint.sum())                    # log evidence
post = joint / joint.sum()                      # p(z | x)

def elbo(q):
    return np.sum(q * (np.log(joint) - np.log(q)))

def kl(q, r):
    return np.sum(q * np.log(q / r))

for name, q in [("prior", prior), ("uniform", np.full(3, 1 / 3)), ("posterior", post)]:
    print(f"{name:9s} ELBO {elbo(q):.4f}  log p(x) {log_px:.4f}  gap {round(log_px - elbo(q), 4) + 0.0:.4f}  KL(q || posterior) {round(kl(q, post), 4) + 0.0:.4f}")
```

```text title="Output"
prior     ELBO -1.5453  log p(x) -1.2379  gap 0.3075  KL(q || posterior) 0.3075
uniform   ELBO -1.4094  log p(x) -1.2379  gap 0.1715  KL(q || posterior) 0.1715
posterior ELBO -1.2379  log p(x) -1.2379  gap 0.0000  KL(q || posterior) 0.0000
```

Every $q$ gives a lower bound; the gap is exactly the KL to the posterior; with $q$ equal to the posterior the
bound is exact. (`+ 0.0` after rounding just turns a printed `-0.0000` into `0.0000`.) Rearranging the ELBO gives
the form you will see in the VAE paper:

$$
\text{ELBO} = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q}[\log p_\theta(x \mid z)] - D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z) \,\|\, p(z)\big)
$$

a reconstruction term minus a KL that keeps $q$ close to the prior. In a VAE, $q$ depends on $x$ through an encoder
network, $p_\theta(x \mid z)$ is a decoder, and the KL term is the closed-form Gaussian KL above.

## Summary

- $H(X \mid Y) = H(X, Y) - H(Y)$. Mutual information $I(X; Y) = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X, Y) = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(x, y) \,\|\, p(x)p(y))$:
  symmetric, $\ge 0$, zero exactly under independence.
- KL $\ge 0$ follows from Jensen for $\log$. KL is asymmetric, additive over independent factors, and has a
  closed form between Gaussians; it can be estimated by Monte Carlo as $\mathbb{E}_p[\log p - \log q]$.
- Forward KL penalises assigning too little mass where the target has mass. Reverse KL penalises assigning
  mass where the target has little, so a restricted approximation may concentrate around one mode.
- Maximum likelihood minimises empirical cross-entropy. Its population counterpart minimises forward KL from data to model.
- ELBO: $\log p(x) \ge \mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x, z) - \log q(z)]$, with gap $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p(z \mid x))$;
  equivalently reconstruction minus $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, \text{prior})$.
